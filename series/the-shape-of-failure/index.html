The Shape of Failure

Updated October 31, 2026 · 8-Part Engineering Series + AI/LLM Appendix

A field guide to the probability, queueing and reliability mechanics hiding inside distributed systems—and to the architectural decisions that keep those systems safe when our assumptions are wrong.

XKCD 2347: Dependency. Every system diagram has a few boxes it would rather not talk about.

“For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled.”

— Richard Feynman, Physicist

No magic numbers. No architecture by optimism.

A system can look healthy in a dashboard and still be one unlucky workload away from an incident. Averages hide tails. Replicas share dependencies. Queues turn small traffic changes into large latency changes. One tenant can consume the capacity that ten others thought they had paid for.

This series follows those failures from their physical causes to the mathematics that can help us reason about them. It is not a catalogue of distributions, and it is not an argument that every engineering problem needs a statistical model. The model is useful only when it explains a real mechanism and changes a real decision.

That discipline matters even more in an era of AI-assisted and agent-generated software. Code that passes a test is not automatically maintainable software; an API that returns HTTP 200 is not automatically a correct system. Engineers still need to explain failure, verify changes and preserve correctness over time.

Across the series, we return to one evolving example: a multi-tenant analytics gateway serving queries through caches, workers, queues and object storage. Each chapter exposes a different way that apparently healthy components can combine into an unhealthy system.

Follow the evidence

  1. Observe: Start with traces, metrics, logs and real workload evidence.
  2. Explain: Identify the physical mechanism generating the behavior.
  3. Define: State the random variable, population, time window and unit of analysis.
  4. Model: Choose the simplest plausible model and write down its assumptions.
  5. Validate: Test fit, residuals, uncertainty and behavior outside the training window.
  6. Decide: Translate the result into capacity, isolation, admission, reliability or cost controls.
  7. Revisit: Detect drift and revise the model when the system changes.

The Golden Rule: A distribution is a model, not an invariant. A pretty curve is not proof—and a model that cannot change an engineering decision is probably not earning its keep.

One system. Eight dimensions of failure.

The chapters build on one another. We start with uncertainty, then follow a request through component failures, queues, fan-out, skew and feedback loops. Finally, we test whether our models deserve to be trusted and extend the same discipline to AI systems.

The engineering series

  • Part 1 · Uncertainty

    The Illusion of Determinism

    Why Capacity Plans Fail When They Treat Workloads as Constants

    Covers: Random variables, workload distributions, capacity reserves, forecast uncertainty, Value at Risk and the economics of sizing systems under uncertain demand.

    The architectural question: What evidence supports our capacity assumptions—and how much financial and operational risk are we accepting when demand differs from the forecast?
  • Part 2 · Reliability

    Reliability, Redundancy, and the Independence Delusion

    Why Healthy Components Can Still Produce an Unreliable System

    Covers: Bernoulli and Binomial models, series and parallel systems, common-cause failures, retry models, idempotency and Weibull lifetime analysis.

    The architectural question: What happens when a workflow depends on many components—or when replicas that look independent share a database, network, identity provider or power domain?
  • Part 3 · Queues

    The Physics of Time, Traffic, and Queues

    Poisson Arrivals, Bursts, and the Nonlinear Cost of Saturation

    Covers: Poisson processes, exponential inter-arrival times, Little's Law, utilization, variability, Kingman's GI/G/1 mean-wait approximation and multi-stage pipelines.

    The architectural question: How should we bound queues, isolate workloads and apply admission control when average utilization hides bursts and service-time variability?
  • Part 4 · Tail risk

    Aggregation and the Extreme Tail

    Why the Slowest Dependency Can Own Your p99

    Covers: Sequential latency sums, parallel fan-out maxima, order statistics, mixtures, normal and log-normal models, and Extreme Value Theory for threshold exceedances.

    The architectural question: How do fan-out width, dependence and rare slow paths affect end-to-end deadlines—and when is a tail estimate too uncertain to extrapolate?
  • Part 5 · Skew

    Routing, Sharding, and the Heavy-Tailed Geometry of Data Skew

    Why Evenly Distributed Keys Do Not Guarantee Evenly Distributed Work

    Covers: Multinomial traffic allocation, Zipf-like popularity, Pareto demand, hot keys and partitions, consistent hashing, tenant-aware routing and sampling without replacement.

    The architectural question: Is the bottleneck caused by key popularity, tenant demand, partition placement or time-correlated bursts—and which control addresses that mechanism?
  • Part 6 · Feedback loops

    When Failure Feeds on Itself

    Retries, Jitter, Whale Tenants, and the Mechanics of Cascading Overload

    Covers: State-dependent retries, bounded and full jitter, retry budgets, backpressure, Pareto-like tenant demand, Beta models for uncertain failure rates and positive feedback loops.

    The architectural question: How do we stop retries and uneven demand from amplifying an incident—and how do we distinguish a transient fault from a system already in overload?
  • Part 7 · Model validation

    The Architect's Reality Check

    How to Challenge a Model Before Production Does

    Covers: Model selection, goodness-of-fit, residual analysis, confidence and credible intervals, stationarity, regime changes, rolling-window drift detection and hard capacity boundaries.

    The architectural question: What evidence would disprove our model, how will we detect that it has drifted, and what safe behavior remains when its assumptions stop holding?
Beyond infrastructure · The AI/LLM frontier

AI systems inherit the familiar problems of distributed systems—queues, dependencies, retries, resource contention and tail latency—but add new uncertainty around retrieval, generation, evaluation and multi-step decisions.

A request can return HTTP 200, valid JSON and a fluent answer while still being factually wrong. Serving reliability is not semantic correctness, and successful execution is not successful task completion. The final part applies the series' modelling discipline to both sides of that boundary.

The AI/LLM frontier

    The Stochastic Shape of AI Systems

    From Token Sampling and Inference Queues to Silent Semantic Failure

    Covers: Categorical token sampling, inference arrival and service behavior, TTFT and inter-token latency, KV-cache pressure, retrieval quality, tool-call reliability, agent workflow composition and semantic evaluation.

    The architectural question: How do we distinguish a serving failure, a contract failure and a plausible-but-wrong answer—and design verification, escalation and recovery for each?
  • Engineering appendix AI / LLM

    Mapping the Shape of Failure to AI Infrastructure

    A Practical Reference for Inference, Retrieval, Agents, and Evaluation

    Covers: Candidate models and measurable variables across inference serving, retrieval, generation, tool execution, agent workflows and automated evaluation—alongside the assumptions and controls each requires.

    How to use it: Treat this as a design-review aid, not a lookup table. Choose a model because the mechanism and evidence support it, not simply because the system uses an LLM.

The point is not to predict perfectly

Probability does not remove uncertainty. It makes uncertainty explicit enough to reason about. The goal is not to find a curve that explains every observation; it is to understand the mechanism, quantify what can be quantified, expose what remains unknown and build controls around the limits.

Design systems that remain safe when your predictions are wrong.
