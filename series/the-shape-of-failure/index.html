The Shape of Failure

Updated October 17, 2026 · 7-Part Series + AI/LLM Frontier

XKCD 2347: Why your p99 latency just spiked to infinity.

“For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled.”

— Richard Feynman, Physicist

No TL;DR. Just The "Don't Guess" Method.

Not everything can be compressed into a three-bullet elevator pitch for your product manager. The devil is in the details, and I fully expect you to experience the devil. If you want a quick summary to pretend you understand systems mathematics, go ask an LLM.

If you want to actually build things that don't collapse under load, grab a coffee. Math isn't a band-aid for bad architecture. We are entering an era where entire systems are churned out of AI "dark factories." The code is generated, the factory is unplugged, and the business collects its bonus for shipping fast. But generating working software is not the same as producing software that remains maintainable.

When production inevitably exhibits bizarre, non-linear behavior six months later, detecting the failure will be easy—but understanding it will be impossible because no human left understands the physics of the system. Every production system must be evaluated not only by how efficiently it operates, but by how reliably engineers can explain its failures, verify its changes, and maintain its correctness over time.

To reason about a system you didn't hand-write, follow the evidence:

  1. Observe: Real telemetry, not vendor promises.
  2. Explain: Find the actual mechanism of chaos.
  3. Model: Pick math. Log assumptions to mock later.
  4. Validate: Does reality agree with your spreadsheet?
  5. Decide: Fix an SLO or save actual money.
  6. Revisit: Spot the flaw before production does.

The Golden Rule: A pretty curve isn't proof. Pick the simplest math that works and move on.

Series Contents

  • Part 1

    The Illusion of Determinism

    Breaking the PowerPoint Architecture Script and Translating Math into FinOps

    Covers: Why deterministic capacity narratives break down under variable demand; defining random variables, forecast distributions, capacity reserves, and financial risk measures (VaR).

    What you'll learn: Load tests provide evidence about tested conditions; they do not prove behavior under every future workload.
  • Part 2

    The Mathematics of Reliability and Retries

    Why Quorums Lie to You, and the Statistical Truth About Node Failures

    Covers: Bernoulli and Binomial reliability models, Geometric retry models, Negative Binomial counts, and Weibull lifetime models.

    What you'll learn: Connect circuit breakers, idempotency, and retry budgets to realistic failure mechanisms. Treat independence as an assumption to test, not a default truth.
  • Part 3

    The Physics of Time, Traffic, and Queues

    Poisson Bursts, Pipeline Delays, and Kingman's Saturation Trap

    Covers: Poisson and exponential models, Kingman's G/G/1 mean-wait approximation, Erlang models, and multi-stage pipeline behavior.

    What you'll learn: Understand why queueing delay rises sharply as utilization approaches capacity. There is no universal 85% failure threshold.
  • Part 4

    Aggregation and the Extreme Tail

    The Flaw of Averages, Compounding Latency, and Extreme Value Theory

    Covers: Normal approximations, log-normal as a candidate for multiplicative effects, and Gumbel/Generalized Pareto models for threshold exceedances.

    What you'll learn: Distinguish the distribution of individual observations from the distribution of sample means. EVT does not identify an absolute worst case by itself.
  • Part 5

    Routing, Sharding, and Data Skew

    The Probability of Load Balancing, Consistent Hashing, and Cache Admissions

    Covers: Multinomial traffic splits, Dirichlet models, Zipf-like popularity for skewed demand, and Hypergeometric sampling without replacement.

    What you'll learn: Diagnose hot partitions and test whether skew changes admission or sharding. Do not assume every workload is Zipfian.
  • Part 6

    Chaos, Jitter, and Whale Tenants

    Managing Thundering Herds, Cascading Failures, and the 80/20 Rule

    Covers: Bounded uniform jitter for de-synchronization, Pareto distributions for heavy-tailed demand, Beta models, and cascading feedback mechanisms.

    What you'll learn: Prevent retry synchronization and tenant monopolization using bounded jitter, admission control, and backpressure.
  • Part 7

    The Architect's Reality Check

    Cognitive Biases, System Laws, and Proving the Math in Production

    Covers: Organizational decision traps, goodness-of-fit tests, residual analysis, stationarity checks, and rolling-window drift detection.

    What you'll learn: Turns statistical analysis into a repeatable production practice. A goodness-of-fit test is evidence, not proof.
The AI/LLM Frontier

The same probability and queueing tools can help analyze LLM serving and autonomous applications, but the mapping is not automatic. As autonomous agents start writing code and operating infrastructure, the requirement for mathematical rigor increases, not decreases.

A system operated by an AI must still be explainable by a human. Detecting that an agent failed is trivial; mathematically understanding why it failed—and ensuring its future changes are verifiable—is the actual engineering challenge.

  • Appendix AI / LLM

    A Distribution-by-Distribution Translation Guide for Inference, Agents, and RAG

    Covers: A mapping of candidate models to AI/LLM variables, observable metrics, validation methods, and the engineering controls required to maintain system correctness.

    What you'll learn: Use the appendix as a decision aid to verify changes and enforce correctness—not a lookup table that prescribes a distribution merely because a system uses an LLM.
