State, Empiricism, and the Simulation Fallacy

November 14, 2026 · Part 6 of 8

“The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results.”

— Often attributed to Albert Einstein (But probably Rita Mae Brown)

Exponential backoff: politely waiting slightly longer before aggressively contributing to a DDoS attack on your own infrastructure.

The Geometric distribution models retries until success. But retries usually break independence—if you retry because a database is down, your retry makes it more down. Markov Models dictate circuit breaker state machines (CLOSED → OPEN → HALF-OPEN). Negative Binomial helps you distinguish a human mistyping a credit card from a botnet blasting combinations. And Beta distributions keep you honest when you only have 50 data points.

1. The Geometric Distribution (The Retry Loop)

The Intuition: The Infinite Loop

Think about the last time you tried to connect to a flaky Wi-Fi network. You click "Connect." It fails. You click again. It fails. You click again. The Geometric distribution models the number of trials required to get the first success.

In distributed systems, this is your retry logic. Every time you hit a 500 error, you flip a coin: Success (1) or Fail (0). The Geometric distribution tells you how many attempts it will take. The problem? Retries are not independent. If the database is down, it stays down. Your retries just make it more down.

The Math

The probability of needing exactly k trials to get the first success, where p is the probability of success on any given trial:

P(X = k) = (1 - p)k-1 · p
Let's decode this:
  • k: The total number of attempts.
  • p: The probability of success on any single attempt.
  • (1 - p)k-1: The probability of failing k-1 times in a row before finally succeeding.

Worked Example: If your service has a 50% success rate (p=0.5), the probability that you need exactly 3 attempts is 0.5^2 * 0.5 = 0.125 (12.5%). If your success rate is only 10%, the probability of needing 3 attempts jumps to 0.9^2 * 0.1 = 0.081.

The SRE Trap

The Geometric distribution assumes that every attempt is independent of the last. In reality, failures are highly correlated (a.k.a. the thundering herd). If 1,000 clients all retry at the exact same millisecond, the success probability p drops dramatically for everyone.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Congestion Collapse" 1986 Internet

In 1986, the early Internet experienced total congestion collapse. Routers dropped packets due to overload; endpoints responded by treating the drops as random and immediately retransmitting. They didn't realize they were part of a feedback loop. Throughput dropped by a factor of 1,000.

Reference: Jacobson, "Congestion Avoidance and Control" (1988)
The "Retry Storm" AWS SDK

The AWS SDK retries failed requests 3 times by default. When S3 has a minor hiccup, millions of clients retry simultaneously, turning a small blip into a massive outage. This is why AWS introduced the "Standard" retry mode with exponential backoff and jitter.

Reference: AWS Architecture Blog: Exponential Backoff and Jitter
The "Circuit Breaker" Netflix Hystrix

Netflix's Hystrix library models retries as a state machine. After N consecutive failures, the circuit opens and stops retrying entirely. This breaks the Geometric distribution by deliberately setting p = 0.

Reference: Netflix Hystrix Wiki

2. The Negative Binomial Distribution (The Fraud Bot)

The Intuition: Racing Failures to the Finish Line

If Geometric is "how many tries until one success," Negative Binomial is "how many failures will I rack up before I get r successes."

In fraud prevention, we use this to distinguish a human mistyping a CVV from a botnet blasting combinations. A human will usually get it right after 1-2 failures. A botnet will rack up 1,000 failures before it gets 10 successes. The shape of the failure distribution is radically different.

The Math

The probability of observing exactly k failures before achieving r successes, where p is the probability of success:

P(X = k) = (k + r - 1 choose k) · (1 - p)k · pr
Let's decode this:
  • r: The target number of successes you are waiting for.
  • k: The number of failures observed.
  • p: The probability of success on any single attempt.
  • (k + r - 1 choose k): The number of ways to arrange those failures and successes.

Worked Example: You want to see 5 successful logins (r=5). The system has a 30% success rate for suspicious IPs (p=0.3). What's the probability of seeing exactly 10 failures first? The math gives roughly 8.4%. If you see 100 failures, the probability drops to near zero.

The SRE Trap

Negative Binomial assumes failures are independent. If an attacker is doing credential stuffing, they are hitting thousands of different accounts. Each failure doesn't decrease their probability of success on the next account. You need to model the failure rate at the tenant level, not the request level.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Velocity Check" Stripe Radar

Stripe uses statistical models to distinguish a user who forgot their password from a botnet. A human might fail 3 times and then succeed. A botnet will fail 1,000 times and only succeed 2 times. The ratio of failures to successes is the tell.

Reference: Stripe Radar Fraud Detection
The "Consumer Lag" Apache Kafka

Kafka consumers retry failed messages. If a poison message fails repeatedly, the consumer can rack up thousands of retries before the DLQ (Dead Letter Queue) kicks in. Negative Binomial models the expected backlog of poison messages.

Reference: Kafka Consumer Configuration
The "Canary Analysis" Spinnaker

When rolling out a canary, Spinnaker analyzes the number of failures before achieving a statistically significant number of successes. If the failure count exceeds the Negative Binomial threshold, it automatically rolls back.

Reference: Spinnaker Canary Analysis

3. The Beta Distribution (The Confidence Trap)

The Intuition: "60% Sure. Ask me again after one more data point."

You run an A/B test. Version A gets 10 clicks out of 20. Version B gets 12 clicks out of 20. Which is better? The Beta distribution models the probability of probabilities. It tells you how confident you should be in your conversion rate.

When you have very little data, the Beta distribution is wide and flat. It says "Your conversion rate could be anywhere from 10% to 90%." As you collect more data, the distribution narrows and sharpens. It's the mathematically correct way to say "I don't know yet."

The Math

The probability density function for a Beta distribution, defined by shape parameters α (successes + 1) and β (failures + 1):

f(x; α, β) = (xα-1 · (1-x)β-1) / B(α, β)
Let's decode this:
  • α (Alpha): The number of observed successes + 1 (your "prior" successes).
  • β (Beta): The number of observed failures + 1 (your "prior" failures).
  • B(α, β): The Beta function, a normalizing constant to make the area under the curve equal 1.

Worked Example: You test a new feature with 4 successes and 8 failures. Your α = 5 and β = 9. The mean of the Beta distribution is 5 / (5+9) = 35.7%. But the variance is high. The true conversion rate could be anywhere from 15% to 60%.

The SRE Trap

If you deploy a canary and it serves 100 requests with 0 errors, you might assume the error rate is 0%. The Beta distribution disagrees. With α=1 and β=101, the 95th percentile of the error rate is still ~3%. You cannot prove a system is safe with a small sample size.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "A/B Test" Netflix / Growth

Netflix uses Bayesian A/B testing to decide which artwork gets the most clicks. They model the click-through rate as a Beta distribution, constantly updating the posterior as new data comes in.

Reference: Netflix Experimentation Platform
The "Blast Radius" Chaos Engineering

When injecting a failure, you want to be 95% confident that the blast radius is less than 1% of traffic. The Beta distribution tells you how many requests you need to test to achieve that confidence.

Reference: Principles of Chaos Engineering
The "Cold Start" Thompson Sampling

Multi-armed bandit algorithms use the Beta distribution to balance exploration (trying new arms) and exploitation (using the best-known arm). It's how recommendation engines avoid getting stuck in a local maximum.

Reference: Thompson Sampling

4. The Triangular / PERT Distribution (The Human Estimate)

The Intuition: The Planning Fallacy

You ask an engineer how long a migration will take. They say, "Best case, 2 days. Worst case, 10 days. Most likely, 4 days." This is a Triangular distribution. It is the mathematical representation of human optimism.

The PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) distribution is a smoothed version of the Triangular distribution. It weighs the "Most Likely" estimate more heavily, reflecting the fact that human estimates are usually clustered around a mode, but we acknowledge the long tail of delays.

The Math

The PERT distribution is defined by three parameters: a (optimistic), m (most likely), and b (pessimistic). The mean of the PERT distribution is heavily weighted toward m:

Mean = (a + 4m + b) / 6
Let's decode this:
  • a: The absolute best-case scenario (e.g., 2 days).
  • m: The most likely scenario (e.g., 4 days).
  • b: The absolute worst-case scenario (e.g., 10 days).

Worked Example: If a=2, m=4, and b=10, the PERT mean is (2 + 16 + 10) / 6 = 4.67 days. Notice how the 10-day worst case pulls the mean from 4 to 4.67. The PERT distribution mathematically penalizes you for having a long tail.

The SRE Trap

Engineers systematically underestimate the worst case (b). They assume "nothing will go wrong." The PERT distribution is only as good as the b you give it. If your worst case is actually 30 days, and you put 10, your entire project plan is mathematically doomed.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Story Points" Jira / Agile

Story points are essentially PERT estimates in disguise. When a team says "This is a 3, but could be an 8," they are implicitly defining the a, m, and b parameters.

Reference: Atlassian: Agile Estimation
The "Incident Recovery" Post-Mortem

During a major incident, the Incident Commander asks "How long until we restore service?" The PERT distribution is used to give a realistic estimate (e.g., "Optimistically 15 mins, realistically 45, worst case 2 hours").

Reference: PagerDuty Incident Response
The "Cost Estimation" FinOps

When migrating to the cloud, finance teams use PERT to estimate the monthly bill. If they only use the "Most Likely" figure, they will blow their budget when the tail event (egress fees, over-provisioning) hits.

Reference: FinOps Foundation
