State, Empiricism, and the Simulation Fallacy

November 14, 2026 · Part 6 of 8

“The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results. In distributed systems, we call this a Default Retry Policy.”

— Every Staff Engineer at 3:14 AM

Several models in earlier chapters begin with a useful simplifying assumption: a probability or arrival rate is stable over the interval being studied. That is a baseline, not a law of production. In a live system, one failed request can change the conditions faced by the next request.

When a primary database begins timing out on lock contention, upstream services may retry. Those retries consume connections, CPU, queue slots, and network capacity. Exponential backoff can spread attempts over time, but without jitter, deadlines, and a retry budget it can still synchronize clients and extend the overload.

We also rarely know the true parameters of a system when making high-stakes decisions. A canary may show 0.0% errors after 50 requests without establishing that its production failure probability is near zero. A queue may receive bursts rather than smooth arrivals. And a project estimate assembled by summing the “most likely” duration of every task ignores both skew and dependency structure. A Monte Carlo simulation does not repair those assumptions; it only propagates them.

Part 6 focuses on retry feedback, state-dependent probabilities, overdispersed counts, and uncertainty in the parameters we use for planning. The recurring rule is simple: a distribution describes a model under assumptions; it does not make those assumptions true.

1. The Geometric Distribution (The Retry Baseline)

The Intuition: Repeated Attempts Under a Fixed Probability

Picture reconnecting to a congested service. The first attempt times out; a later attempt may succeed. The geometric distribution is the baseline model for the number of Bernoulli attempts up to the first success when each attempt has the same success probability and attempts are independent.

For a retrying client, this is a useful starting point—not a complete production model. If retries consume connections, CPU, or queue capacity, the system state changes and the next attempt may face a different success probability. We will model that feedback separately rather than pretending a constant-probability formula captures it.

The Math & Multi-Tier Work Amplification

Let \( M \) be the maximum number of total attempts, including the original request. Under the constant-probability baseline, each attempt succeeds independently with probability \( p \), and fails with probability \( q=1-p \). The probability that the first success occurs on attempt \( k \), for \( k=1,\ldots,M \), and the probability of exhausting the cap are:

\[ P(N_M=k)=q^{k-1}p,\quad k=1,\ldots,M;\qquad P(\text{failure after }M)=q^M \]
\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[N_M] &=\sum_{k=1}^{M} kq^{k-1}p + Mq^M\\ &=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}q^j =\frac{1-q^M}{1-q} =\frac{1-(1-p)^M}{p},\quad p>0. \end{aligned}\]

At the boundaries, use the limits directly: if \(p=0\), all \(M\) attempts are consumed and \(\mathbb{E}[N_M]=M\); if \(p=1\), the first attempt succeeds and \(\mathbb{E}[N_M]=1\). The success probabilities across attempts sum to \(1-q^M\); the remaining probability mass \(q^M\) is the terminal failure event. This convention keeps “maximum attempts” distinct from “maximum retries”: \(M=3\) means one initial attempt plus at most two retries.

When the probability changes with system state

The geometric model assumes the same \(p\) on every attempt. A feedback system needs conditional probabilities \(p_k=P(\text{success on attempt }k\mid\text{all earlier attempts failed, current state})\), with \(q_k=1-p_k\). Then:

\[P(\text{first success on }k)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}q_j\right)p_k,\quad P(\text{failure after }M)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}q_j\]
\[\mathbb{E}[N_M]=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\prod_{j=1}^{i}q_j,\quad\text{where the empty product is }1.\]

Illustrative three-attempt example. Suppose success probabilities deteriorate after each failure: \(p_1=0.40\), \(p_2=0.25\), and \(p_3=0.10\). The probabilities of success on attempts 1, 2, and 3 are \(0.400\), \(0.150\), and \(0.045\); final failure is \(0.405\). Expected attempts are \(1+0.60+(0.60)(0.75)=2.05\). If we incorrectly hold the first-attempt probability fixed at \(p=0.40\), the geometric baseline predicts final failure \(0.60^3=0.216\) and expected attempts \(1+0.60+0.60^2=1.96\). The constant-\(p\) model materially understates the chance of exhausting the retry cap in this example.

This conditional model is still a compact description. In a real service, \(p_k\) may depend on queue depth, concurrency, tenant mix, timeout budget, breaker state, and recovery progress. A queueing or state-transition model is needed when those variables feed back materially.

Decoding the architectural blast radius:
  • \( \mathbb{E}[N_M] \) (The Work Amplification Factor): With unbounded, independent retries, a stable \(p=0.99\) gives \(1/p\approx1.01\) expected attempts per logical request. If success probability falls to \(p=0.10\), the unbounded expectation becomes 10 attempts; 1,000 logical requests/s would imply 10,000 attempts/s under that model. This is a baseline calculation, not a production forecast: deadlines, attempt caps, queueing, and changing state alter realised load.
  • \( A_{\text{stack}} \) (Multi-Tier Cascading Amplification): Suppose three nested layers (Gateway → Orchestrator → Storage) each permit at most \(M=3\) total attempts and each attempt has the same independent \(p=0.10\). Each layer has \(E[N_3]=(1-0.9^3)/0.1=2.71\) and final-failure probability \(0.9^3=0.729\). Under the simplified assumption that every upper-layer attempt invokes the next layer and the retry policies are independent, expected storage attempts per original request multiply to \(2.71^3\approx19.9\). This is illustrative, not universal: short-circuiting, shared deadlines, non-retryable errors, and attempt budgets change it.
  • State-Dependent Feedback (The Metastable Trap): During congestion, conditional success probability can fall as queue depth and utilization rise. The accounting identity \(\lambda_{\text{attempt}}=\lambda_0 E[N_M]\) is useful only when the retry process is stationary enough for that expectation to be meaningful. In feedback, use the state-dependent \(p_k\) model or a queueing/state-transition model. Retries can keep queues and worker pools busy after original traffic falls; this is a possible metastable failure mode, not an inevitable consequence of \(\rho\to1\).
The Principal Architect Trap: Attempt Caps vs. Retry Budgets

It is a fatal error to confuse a per-request attempt cap (\( M \)) with an aggregate retry token-bucket budget. An attempt cap bounds a single call to \( M \) tries. A retry budget caps aggregate retry attempts over a defined interval (for example, a token bucket replenished at 10% of the original request rate). It is separate from a per-request cap and should specify both refill rate and burst capacity.

The Numerical Reality Check: Let original traffic be \(\lambda_0=1{,}000\) requests/s, let \(M=4\) total attempts per request, and configure a retry token bucket with a refill rate of 100 retry attempts/s and a burst capacity of 100 tokens.

  • Scenario A (1% Transient Flake): 10 requests fail. They retry, consuming 10 tokens. The budget holds. Load = 1,010 RPS.
  • Scenario B (50% Downstream Outage): 500 requests fail. At most 100 additional attempts are admitted during that second, and the other 400 first-attempt failures are not retried. The admitted attempt rate is therefore about 1,100 attempts/s over that interval, or 1.10× the original attempt rate. This is a rate/burst-budget illustration, not a strict instantaneous peak bound unless the bucket capacity is also enforced at 100 and traffic is measured over the same interval. With the same 50% failure scenario, an attempt cap alone could permit 2,500 attempts/s if all 500 failed requests exhaust four total attempts. Across all 1,000 requests, the absolute cap is 4,000 attempts/s if every request exhausts all four attempts.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The 1986 Congestion Collapse Documented history

In October 1986, throughput on the LBL–UC Berkeley path collapsed from roughly 32 Kbps to 40 bps. The episode helped motivate TCP congestion avoidance: retransmissions and growing offered load could worsen congestion rather than restore throughput. It is not evidence that TCP endpoints literally implemented a geometric retry model; the geometric distribution here is an explanatory baseline for repeated attempts, while congestion control addresses the feedback mechanism.

Reference: Jacobson, "Congestion Avoidance and Control" (SIGCOMM 1988)
Why Backoff Needs Jitter Engineering pattern

If many clients time out together, deterministic exponential backoff can preserve their synchronization: they all wait, then retry together. Full jitter chooses a random delay \(D_k\sim U(0,\min(D_{\max},D_0 2^{k-1}))\). Jitter spreads the retry wave; it does not by itself cap total work or guarantee recovery. Pair it with a retry budget, a deadline, and admission control.

Reference: Marc Brooker, "Exponential Backoff And Jitter" (AWS Architecture Blog)

Architectural controls: stop retries from becoming the workload

The controls below address different parts of the feedback loop. None is a replacement for the others:

  • Exponential backoff: increase the delay after each failed attempt, for example with a capped schedule \(d_k=\min(d_{\max},d_0 2^{k-1})\). This reduces immediate pressure but does not reduce the number of attempts by itself.
  • Jitter: randomize each delay, such as full jitter \(D_k\sim U(0,d_k)\), so clients do not retry in synchronized waves. Verify the retry-arrival histogram, not just the configured delay.
  • Retry budgets: limit aggregate retry work with a token bucket. This bounds amplification at the service boundary; the refill rate and burst capacity must be explicit.
  • Circuit breakers: move through closed, open, and half-open states using a defined failure/latency window. In half-open, admit only a small number of probes; do not release the entire waiting population when one probe succeeds.
  • Admission control and load shedding: reject or defer work before queues and worker pools saturate. Shed low-priority or expired work before it consumes scarce downstream capacity.
  • Backpressure: make upstream producers slow down when downstream queues or workers approach their safe limits. Verify that the signal propagates across asynchronous boundaries instead of merely moving the unbounded queue elsewhere.
  • Error classification: retry only failures plausibly transient and safe to retry. Validation, authorization, and deterministic contract errors should fail without consuming retry capacity.
  • Deadline propagation: pass the caller's remaining deadline downstream. Do not start another attempt when the remaining budget cannot cover a useful attempt and its response path.
  • Nested retry prevention: designate one layer to own retries, or propagate a shared attempt/deadline budget. Otherwise independent retry policies multiply work across tiers.
  • Idempotency: use idempotency keys or deduplication for operations with side effects. A timeout does not prove the server failed before committing the operation; a blind retry can duplicate a payment, write, or job.

Why a recovering system can become less stable: a timeout creates a retry; the retry adds queueing and contention; the longer queue causes more timeouts; and those timeouts create more retries. Backoff changes timing, jitter changes synchronization, and a breaker changes admission. The combination must be bounded by deadlines and retry budgets. Measure original requests, retry attempts, queue depth, in-flight work, deadline expiry, breaker state, and idempotency conflicts separately.

2. The Negative Binomial Distribution (Overdispersion & Bursty Counts)

The Intuition: Counting the Bodies Before \( r \) Victories

If the Geometric distribution asks, "How many tries until my first success?", the Negative Binomial distribution asks the broader operational question: "How many failures (\( k \)) will I accumulate before I finally collect \( r \) successes?"

In systems engineering, the failures-before-\(r\)-successes form is a candidate model for repeated comparable trials, such as an illustrative sequence of authorization declines before a fixed number of approvals. For counts across time windows with a fluctuating event rate, the Gamma–Poisson mixture gives a related Negative Binomial model. Neither formulation automatically explains correlated poison batches; the physical mechanism must match the model's assumptions.

The Math & The Multi-Tenant Superposition Trap

Here \(X\) counts failures before the \(r\)-th success, with independent Bernoulli trials and a constant success probability \(p\). This is one common Negative Binomial convention. If instead \(T=X+r\) counts total attempts up to and including the \(r\)-th success, its mean increases by \(r\) while its variance is unchanged:

\[ P(X = k) = \binom{k + r - 1}{k} (1-p)^k p^r \]
\[\begin{aligned} E[X]&=\frac{r(1-p)}{p}, & \operatorname{Var}(X)&=\frac{r(1-p)}{p^2},\\ E[T]&=\frac{r}{p}, & \operatorname{Var}(T)&=\frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}. \end{aligned}\]
Fixed-rate superposition vs. random intensity:
  • The Superposition Principle: A whale tenant plus many small tenants does not, by itself, create a Negative Binomial count. If each tenant generates an independent Poisson process with a fixed rate \(\lambda_i\), their superposition is still Poisson with rate \(\sum_i\lambda_i\), and its count variance equals its mean. Overdispersion requires something additional, such as random or time-varying intensity, dependence, or an unmodelled mixture.
  • The Gamma-Poisson Mixture (Cox Process): Overdispersion emerges only when the arrival intensity \( \Lambda(t) \) is itself a stochastic variable across time windows (e.g., top-of-the-hour batch cron jobs). When \(\Lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(r,\theta)\) with shape \(r\) and scale \(\theta\), mixing \(\operatorname{Poisson}(\Lambda)\) produces a Negative Binomial count with mean \(\mu=r\theta\) and variance \(\mu+\mu^2/r\). Under the failures-before-successes parameterization above, \(p=1/(1+\theta)\), so the variance-to-mean ratio is \(1/p>1\).
  • The Dispersion Parameter (\( r \)): In the mean–dispersion parameterization \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mu+\mu^2/r\), taking \(r\to\infty\) while holding \(\mu\) fixed approaches the Poisson limit. Taking \(r\) small at fixed \(\mu\) produces strong overdispersion. Do not infer the physical cause from a fitted distribution alone; changing intensity, mixtures, and dependence can all produce extra-Poisson variation.
The SRE Trap: Request-Level vs. Tenant-Level Velocity

When defending against credential stuffing or card testing, engineers often rate-limit individual client IPs. Distributed botnets can spread attempts across proxy networks, making per-IP velocity limits insufficient on their own. The approval probability is not a universal constant: it depends on the credential population, merchant controls, and attacker targeting. An illustrative detector can model declines before a chosen number of approvals, aggregated across signals such as account, device, network, or merchant. That model is useful only if trials are sufficiently comparable; adaptive attackers and changing controls make \(p\) state-dependent. A Negative Binomial score is not a guaranteed immediate detector and should be combined with velocity limits and risk signals.

Scenarios & Case Studies

Distributed Card-Testing Detection Illustrative model

Card-testing attacks use repeated low-value authorization attempts across accounts and network identities. A merchant could evaluate a candidate count model for declines before a chosen number of approvals and combine it with device, network, and account signals to trigger step-up verification or throttling. This is an illustrative application of the distribution, not a claim that a named fraud product uses this exact model.

Context: Stripe documentation on card-testing prevention
Poison-Pill Amplification in Micro-Batches Kafka / PySpark

When a streaming consumer reads micro-batches of 1,000 events and one payload is malformed, naive batch-level retries can replay the entire batch. Per-record Dead-Letter Queue (DLQ) routing can isolate the poison record and let valid records progress. This is a batch-processing failure pattern, not automatically a Negative Binomial process; the distribution applies only if the chosen count variable and trial assumptions fit.

Reference: Confluent: Error Handling and Dead Letter Queues in Apache Kafka

3. The Beta Distribution (Why Your 100% Canary Is Lying to You)

The Intuition: Modeling the Probability of a Probability

Imagine choosing between two restaurants online. Restaurant A has a 5.0-star rating (from 2 reviews). Restaurant B has a 4.8-star rating (from 1,800 reviews). You pick Restaurant B every time, because your brain instinctively performs Bayesian inference: 2 out of 2 could easily be the owner's immediate family.

Yet in production, teams deploy a canary pod, watch it serve 50 requests with 0 errors, see Success Rate: 100%, and promote the build globally. A Beta distribution can represent epistemic uncertainty about an unknown Bernoulli success or failure probability. It does not make a small sample representative, and it does not remove the need to check whether requests are comparable.

The Math: Bayesian Credible Bounds vs. Frequentist Rule of Three

The Beta distribution has support \([0,1]\) and positive shape parameters \(\alpha,\beta\). For Bernoulli observations with a stable unknown success probability, a \(\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0,\beta_0)\) prior updates after \(s\) successes and \(f\) failures to \(\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0+s,\beta_0+f)\). When the parameter of interest is the failure probability, count observed errors in the first shape parameter: the posterior is \(\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0+f,\beta_0+s)\).

\[ f(x; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{\text{B}(\alpha, \beta)} \quad \text{and} \quad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)} \]
\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \quad \text{and} \quad p_{\text{upper},95\%}(f=0) \approx \frac{3}{n} \]
Zero Failures in \( n = 50 \) Canary Trials (\( k = 0 \)): Three Perspectives
  • Bayesian Credible Upper Bound (uniform prior on failure probability, \(\text{Beta}(1,1)\)): For the error probability, conjugate updating gives \(\alpha'=\alpha_0+f\) and \(\beta'=\beta_0+s\). Observing 0 failures and 50 successes shifts the posterior to \(\text{Beta}(1,51)\). Solving the CDF \( F(p) = 1 - (1-p)^{51} = 0.95 \) gives \( p_{95} = 1 - (0.05)^{1/51} = \) 5.70%.
  • Frequentist Exact One-Sided 95% Clopper-Pearson: Inverting Binomial tail probabilities \( P(X = 0 \mid p) = (1-p)^{50} \ge 0.05 \) yields \( 1 - (0.05)^{1/50} = \) 5.82%.
  • The Rule of Three (approximation): for zero observed failures, the one-sided 95% upper bound is approximately \(-\ln(0.05)/n\approx2.996/n\), or about 3 / 50 = 6.00%.

Worked Example: If the target failure probability is at most 0.1%, zero failures in 50 independent trials is nowhere near enough: the exact one-sided 95% upper bound is about 5.82%. The exact zero-failure sample-size condition is \((1-p_0)^n\le0.05\); for \(p_0=0.001\), this requires \(n\ge\ln(0.05)/\ln(0.999)\), or 2,995 independent, representative requests. The Rule-of-Three approximation gives 3,000.

Assumptions matter: the simple update treats outcomes as conditionally independent Bernoulli trials sharing one stable probability. Correlated requests from the same tenant, repeated requests to one hot key, time-varying load, or a deployment that changes during the sample violate that simple interpretation. The posterior then overstates the information in the data. Stratify by relevant state, model dependence or time variation, and avoid treating 50 correlated requests as 50 independent experiments.

Architectural Application: Solving the "Half-Open" Thundering Herd

When a node recovers, one successful probe is weak evidence: it does not establish that the node can safely absorb full traffic. A Beta posterior can express uncertainty about its success probability, but routing policy must separately cap exploration. Use a small, bounded half-open probe allowance, ramp traffic gradually, and close the circuit again on defined failure or latency signals. Thompson Sampling is an exploration policy, not a substitute for a safety limit or a circuit breaker.

4. The Beta-PERT Distribution (The Simulation Fallacy & Jensen's Inequality)

The Intuition: Why Summing Most-Likely Estimates Can Mislead

Ask a Staff Engineer how long decommissioning a legacy database will take. They provide three points: "Optimistically, 1 day (\( a \)). Most likely, 2.5 days (\( m \)). Pessimistically, if schemas contain undocumented stored procedures, 9 days (\( b \))."

For a serial chain of 10 tasks, leadership may take the "Most Likely" mode (\(m=2.5\) days), sum the modes to get 25 days, and commit to that deadline. A Beta-PERT model exposes why the mode is not an expected duration: in this right-skewed example, the mean exceeds the mode. Parallel merge points can push completion times further into the tail.

The Math, Exact Variance & Parallel Merge Penalties

The ordinary Beta distribution is a probability distribution on \([0,1]\), often used for uncertainty about a Bernoulli parameter. Beta-PERT is different: it scales a Beta-shaped distribution onto a duration or cost interval \([a,b]\), using optimistic, most-likely, and pessimistic estimates. In the common PERT convention shown here, the mode receives weight 4:

\[ \alpha = 1 + \frac{4(m - a)}{b - a} \quad \text{and} \quad \beta = 1 + \frac{4(b - m)}{b - a} \]
\[ \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6} \]
\[ \text{Var}(T) = (b - a)^2 \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha + \beta)^2 (\alpha + \beta + 1)} \]
Rigorous Calculations: Single-Task P95 vs. 5-Way Parallel Merge
  • Single-Task Exact P95 (\( a=1, m=2.5, b=9 \)): Yields \(\alpha=1.75,\beta=4.25\). The expected duration is \(E[T]=3.33\) days, above the most-likely mode of 2.5 days. Evaluating the inverse-CDF gives \( P_{95} = \) 5.90 days (not 5.6 days).
  • Exact Variance vs. Historical Approximation: The 1958 slide-rule heuristic posits \( \sigma \approx (b-a)/6 \). For \( a=2, m=4, b=12 \) (\( \alpha=1.8, \beta=4.2 \)), the heuristic yields \( \sigma \approx 1.667 \) (\( \text{Var} \approx 2.78 \)), whereas exact Beta variance is 100 × (7.56) / (36 × 7) = 3.000 days2 (\( \sigma = \) 1.732 days).
  • Jensen's Inequality and the Parallel Merge: For a scatter-gather query or 5 parallel microservice migrations (\( a=2, m=4, b=12 \)), the merge node completes at \( T_{\text{total}} = \max(T_1, \dots, T_5) \). Because the max function is convex, Jensen's Inequality gives \( \mathbb{E}[\max(T_1, \dots, T_n)] > \max(\mathbb{E}[T_1], \dots, \mathbb{E}[T_n]) \).
    • At \(T=4\) days, one branch has probability about 0.3316 of completion. If the five task durations are independent and identically distributed, the probability all five are done by Day 4 is \((0.3316)^5\approx\)0.40%. Correlated task risks change this product.
    • For independent, identically distributed branches with CDF \(F_T(t)\), the join CDF is \(P(T_{\max}\le t)=F_T(t)^5\), and \(E[T_{\max}]=\int_a^b[1-F_T(t)^5]dt\). Evaluating this for the stated Beta-PERT inputs gives 7.17 days. Parallel execution is still faster than running all five tasks serially, but the join waits for the slowest branch; one long task can dominate its tail.

Beta-PERT is an elicitation model, not a fitted guarantee. Its three inputs are judgments or planning bounds; the smooth curve between them is an assumption. Monte Carlo draws can quantify the consequences of those inputs, but cannot validate that the inputs, task network, or dependence structure are correct. If two tasks share a vendor, deployment window, team, or unknown dependency, model their common risk rather than sampling them as independent.

The Principal Architect Takeaway: Stop Giving Point Estimates

For RTO scenarios, cloud FinOps forecasts, or migrations, do not present a single point estimate as if it were certain. Elicit three-point estimates \((a,m,b)\), model the dependency graph and relevant correlations, and report at least the mean (expected duration) and P50 (median), plus a decision-relevant P90 or P95. A percentile is not automatically a commitment guarantee; it is conditional on the model and inputs. Summing modes is generally not an estimate of the expected total duration.

5. The Principal Architect's Synthesis

System failures under production load frequently trace back to replacing skewed, stateful distributions with point estimates. Use this diagnostic matrix during architectural design reviews:

Distribution The Naive Assumption The Production Reality Architectural Defense
Bounded geometric
(1 − q^M) / p		 Every retry sees the same probability and costs nothing. Under constant \(p\), expected attempts are bounded by \(M\); state-dependent failure can make the baseline optimistic. Retry budgets, exponential backoff with jitter, one retry-owning layer, deadlines, circuit breakers, admission control, and idempotency.
Negative Binomial
Var(X) = μ + μ²/r		 Counts are Poisson with one fixed intensity. Random intensity across windows can produce overdispersion; fixed-rate Poisson superposition alone does not. Per-record DLQs, tenant velocity controls, and queue headroom based on measured burst behaviour.
Beta posterior
α / (α + β)		 Zero observed failures means the true failure probability is zero. With 50 independent zero-failure trials, the one-sided 95% upper bound is still about 5.8%. Bayesian bounds or exact binomial bounds, representative sampling, bounded canary probes, and state-aware interpretation.
Beta-PERT
(a + 4m + b) / 6		 The most-likely duration is the expected duration. Skew and dependency-graph merge points change the distribution of completion time. Three-point estimates and Monte Carlo with explicit dependency and correlation assumptions; choose a percentile based on risk tolerance.

The lesson is not to abandon probability models; it is to stop confusing a tractable baseline with a faithful description of a live system.
