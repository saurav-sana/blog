State, Empiricism, and the Simulation Fallacy

November 14, 2026 · Part 6 of 8

“The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results. In distributed systems, we call this a Default Retry Policy.”

— Every Staff Engineer at 3:14 AM

Up until now in this series, we have indulged in a comforting textbook fiction: that the universe is memoryless, that probabilities are fixed constants handed down from Mount Olympus, and that one failed packet does not conspire with the next. We treated our servers like casino roulette wheels—stateless, stationary, and completely indifferent to how desperately the gambler needs to win.

Production infrastructure does not behave like a roulette wheel. When a primary database begins timing out on lock contention, your upstream microservices do not shrug and walk away; they enter a retry loop. And exponential backoff is usually just politely waiting 200 milliseconds before aggressively contributing to a distributed denial-of-service attack on your own infrastructure.

Even worse, we rarely know the true parameters of our systems when making high-stakes decisions. We promote canary deployments after 50 requests because the dashboard says 0.0% errors. We size Kafka Dead-Letter Queues assuming tenant failures arrive smoothly rather than in correlated poison batches. And we promise executives delivery dates by summing up "most likely" Jira estimates—a mathematical felony known as the Simulation Fallacy.

In Part 6, we abandon static, known probabilities and confront stateful feedback loops, multi-tenant overdispersion, and epistemic uncertainty.

1. The Geometric Distribution (The Stateful Retry Storm)

The Intuition: Flipping a Coin That Gets Heavier Every Time You Miss

Picture trying to connect to a congested conference Wi-Fi network. You click Connect. Timeout. You click again. Timeout. You click a third time, and the DHCP handshake finally completes. The Geometric distribution models the exact number of attempts (k) required to achieve your very first success.

In distributed architecture, this is the mathematical skeleton of every retry loop. Every time an HTTP client receives a 503 Service Unavailable, it flips a coin with success probability p. The catch? In a textbook, coin flips are independent. In production, retries consume thread pools, sockets, and CPU cycles—meaning every failed attempt actively degrades p for the next attempt.

The Math & Multi-Tier Work Amplification

Assuming an independent per-attempt success probability p (a dangerous assumption we will dismantle shortly), the probability of requiring exactly k total attempts (k−1 failures followed by 1 success) is:

P(X = k) = (1 − p)k−1 · p
|
E[X] = 1 − (1 − p)Mp
Decoding the architectural blast radius:
  • E[X] (The Work Amplification Factor): If your Parquet worker pool is healthy (p = 0.99), each logical query costs 1 / 0.99 = 1.01 physical attempts (assuming unbounded retries). If a GC pause drops p to 0.10, each query demands an average of 10.0 physical attempts. A system built for 1,000 queries per second is suddenly hit with 10,000 queries per second.
  • Astack (Multi-Tier Cascading Amplification): Suppose you configure a bounded retry policy of M = 3 (max 3 total attempts) to be "safe". But you configure that same policy across L = 3 layers of your stack (Gateway → Orchestrator → Storage). The expected attempts per layer at p = 0.10 is (1 - 0.9^3)/0.1 = 2.71. When nested across 3 layers, the database experiences 2.713 ≈ 19.9× load amplification!
The Principal Architect Trap: State-Dependent Decay & Markov Circuit Breakers

In a real outage, p is not static; it transitions through a stateful feedback loop where p decays as a function of the offered load Λ(t). Once offered load crosses the saturation point, the system enters a metastable failure state: even after external traffic returns to normal, the cluster remains pinned at 100% CPU processing its own backlog of retries.

How do we break the Geometric loop? With two stateful defenses:

  • Markov Chain Circuit Breakers (CLOSED → OPEN → HALF-OPEN): Instead of letting clients endlessly sample from a Geometric tail when p → 0, a circuit breaker tracks consecutive failures. Once a threshold is crossed, the state transitions to OPEN, clamping attempt volume to zero (k = 0, fail-fast) for a cooldown window before transitioning to HALF-OPEN to test the waters with single probe requests.
  • Client-Side Retry Budgets: Enforce a token-bucket ratio (e.g., Envoy/Google SRE's 10% rule: retries may never exceed 10% of base traffic volume). This mathematically caps your worst-case Work Amplification Factor at 1.10× instead of 1 / p.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The 1,000x Congestion Collapse 1986 ARPANET

In October 1986, the link between LBL and UC Berkeley dropped from 32 Kbps to 40 bps—a 1,000× collapse. Early TCP endpoints treated dropped packets as memoryless Geometric trials and immediately retransmitted full windows, filling router queues with duplicate zombie packets until Van Jacobson introduced TCP Slow Start and multiplicative backoff.

Reference: Jacobson, "Congestion Avoidance and Control" (SIGCOMM 1988)
Metastable Retry Storms & Full Jitter AWS DynamoDB / S3

During the famous 2015 DynamoDB metadata outage, thousands of storage nodes simultaneously hit lease timeouts and retried against membership servers. Synchronized binary exponential backoff (2k) still collided in phase waves. AWS solved this by combining Full Jitter (sleep ~ U(0, T0 · 2k)) with client-side token-bucket retry throttling.

Reference: Marc Brooker, "Exponential Backoff And Jitter" (AWS Architecture Blog)

2. The Negative Binomial Distribution (Overdispersion & Poison Pills)

The Intuition: Counting the Bodies Before r Victories

If the Geometric distribution asks, "How many tries until my first success?", the Negative Binomial distribution asks the broader operational question: "How many failures (k) will I accumulate before I finally collect r successes?"

In systems engineering, this distribution models two critical phenomena: credential stuffing / card testing (where automated bots cycle thousands of declines to extract valid approvals) and correlated micro-batch failures. Most importantly, it governs queues when arrival intensity fluctuates across observation windows.

The Math & The Multi-Tenant Superposition Trap

The probability of observing exactly k failures before achieving your r-th success, along with its mean and variance, is given by:

P(X = k) = (k + r − 1)!k! (r − 1)! (1 − p)k pr
|
μ = r(1 − p)p
|
σ2 = μ + μ2r
The Superposition Theorem vs. Overdispersed Intensity:
  • The Superposition Principle: Many engineers assume having one "whale tenant" doing 100 RPS and fifty small tenants doing 1 RPS creates a Negative Binomial count. It does not. By the Poisson Superposition Theorem, summing independent Poisson processes with fixed rates λi yields another pure Poisson process (∑ Poisson(λi) = Poisson(∑ λi)), which still strictly maintains equidispersion (σ2 = μ).
  • The Gamma-Poisson Mixture (Cox Process): Overdispersion (σ2 > μ) emerges only when the arrival intensity Λ(t) is itself a stochastic variable across time windows (e.g., top-of-the-hour batch cron jobs). When Λ ~ Gamma(r, θ), mixing Poisson(Λ) produces a Negative Binomial distribution with Variance-to-Mean Ratio (VMR) = 1 + θ = 1/p > 1.
  • The Dispersion Parameter (r): As r → ∞, the quadratic overdispersion term μ2/r vanishes, converging to Poisson behavior. As r → 0, variance explodes into extreme clustering.
The SRE Trap: Request-Level vs. Tenant-Level Velocity

When defending against credential stuffing or card testing, engineers often rate-limit individual client IPs. Botnets bypass this effortlessly by rotating through 50,000 residential proxy IPs—keeping per-IP velocity minuscule. However, the attacker cannot alter the underlying success probability p of their stolen credential dump (typically p < 0.01). By tracking the Negative Binomial ratio of failures (k) to successes (r) aggregated at the Target Tenant / ASN / Device Fingerprint level, bot attacks trigger detection immediately.

Scenarios & Case Studies

Distributed Card-Testing Detection Stripe Radar

Carding botnets validate stolen credit cards by submitting $1.00 authorization checks across distributed proxy networks. By evaluating the Negative Binomial likelihood of observing k declines before r approvals across a merchant's checkout flow, fraud engines automatically trigger CAPTCHAs or step-up 3DS authentication before the botnet finds working cards.

Reference: Stripe Engineering: Preventing Card Testing Attacks
Poison-Pill Amplification in Micro-Batches Kafka / PySpark

When a streaming consumer reads micro-batches of 1,000 events and a single tenant injects malformed payloads, naive batch-level retries replay the entire batch repeatedly before committing r healthy offsets. Implementing per-record Dead-Letter Queue (DLQ) routing decouples poison failures (k) from healthy partition progress (r).

Reference: Confluent: Error Handling and Dead Letter Queues in Apache Kafka

3. The Beta Distribution (Why Your 100% Canary Is Lying to You)

The Intuition: Modeling the Probability of a Probability

Imagine choosing between two restaurants online. Restaurant A has a 5.0-star rating (from 2 reviews). Restaurant B has a 4.8-star rating (from 1,800 reviews). You pick Restaurant B every time, because your brain instinctively performs Bayesian inference: 2 out of 2 could easily be the owner's immediate family.

Yet in production, teams deploy a canary pod, watch it serve 50 requests with 0 errors, see Success Rate: 100%, and promote the build globally. The Beta distribution is the cure for small-sample arrogance: it models epistemic uncertainty over an unknown Bernoulli parameter.

The Math: Bayesian Credible Bounds vs. Frequentist Rule of Three

Governed by shape parameters α (prior + observed successes) and β (prior + observed failures) over [0, 1]:

f(x; α, β) = xα−1 (1 − x)β−1B(α, β)
|
E[X] = αα + β
|
P95, err (k=0) ≈ 3n
Zero Failures in n = 50 Canary Trials (k = 0): Three Perspectives
  • Bayesian Credible Upper Bound (Uniform Prior Beta(1, 1)): Posterior becomes Beta(1, 51). Solving the CDF F(p) = 1 − (1−p)51 = 0.95 gives p95 = 1 − (0.05)1/51 = 5.70%.
  • Frequentist Exact One-Sided 95% Clopper-Pearson: Inverting Binomial tail probabilities P(X = 0 | p) = (1−p)50 ≥ 0.05 yields 1 − (0.05)1/50 = 5.82%.
  • The Rule of Three (Asymptotic Approximation): Taylor expansion of −ln(0.05)/n ≈ 2.996/n yields 3 / 50 = 6.00%.

Worked Example: If your production SLA requires an error budget below 0.1% (Three Nines), observing zero errors in 50 trials establishes only that your failure rate is under ~5.8%. To prove failure probability ≤ 0.001 with 95% confidence requires at least 3 / 0.001 = 3,000 independent canary requests without a single failure.

Architectural Application: Solving the "Half-Open" Thundering Herd

When an unhealthy node recovers, routing based on empirical ratios causes instantaneous flooding because 1 probe / 1 success equals 100%. Maintaining a decaying Beta(αi, βi) score and applying Thompson Sampling—drawing a random variate per request and selecting the maximum—naturally allocates small exploratory traffic to newly recovered instances until variance contracts.

4. The Beta-PERT Distribution (The Simulation Fallacy & Jensen's Inequality)

The Intuition: Why Adding Up "Most Likely" Estimates Guarantees Failure

Ask a Staff Engineer how long decommissioning a legacy database will take. They provide three points: "Optimistically, 1 day (a). Most likely, 2.5 days (m). Pessimistically, if schemas contain undocumented stored procedures, 9 days (b)."

Leadership often takes the "Most Likely" mode (m = 2.5 days), sums 10 tasks to get 25 days, and commits to a deadline. The Beta-PERT distribution exposes why this is mathematically flawed: in right-skewed engineering tasks, the Mean is strictly higher than the Mode, and parallel merge points compound delays non-linearly.

The Math, Exact Variance & Parallel Merge Penalties

Beta-PERT maps three-point estimates onto a smooth Beta curve over [a, b], weighting the mode by 4:

α = 1 + 4m − ab − a,   β = 1 + 4b − mb − a
|
μ = a + 4m + b6
|
Var(T) = (b − a)2 αβ(α + β)2 (α + β + 1)
Rigorous Calculations: Single-Task P95 vs. 5-Way Parallel Merge
  • Single-Task Exact P95 (a=1, m=2.5, b=9): Yields α = 1.75, β = 4.25. Evaluating Ix−1(0.95; 1.75, 4.25) gives x95 ≈ 0.6119. Mapping back to [1, 9] gives P95 = 1 + 8 × 0.6119 = 5.90 days (not 5.6 days).
  • Exact Variance vs. Historical Approximation: The 1958 slide-rule heuristic posits σ ≈ (b−a)/6. For a=2, m=4, b=12 (α=1.8, β=4.2), the heuristic yields σ ≈ 1.667 (Var ≈ 2.78), whereas exact Beta variance is 100 × (7.56) / (36 × 7) = 3.000 days2 (σ = 1.732 days).
  • 5-Way Parallel Merge (a=2, m=4, b=12, where μ=5.0): For a scatter-gather query or 5 parallel squads, the merge node completes at Ttotal = max(T1, …, T5).
    • At T = 4 days (x = 0.2), branch CDF is F(4) = I0.2(1.8, 4.2) ≈ 0.3316. The probability that all 5 finish by Day 4 is (0.3316)5 = 0.00401 ≈ 0.40% (not 0.3%).
    • Integrating the extreme-value order statistic E[max] = 2 + 10 ∫01 (1 − [Ix(1.8, 4.2)]5) dx yields 7.17 days (not 7.4 days).
  • Jensen's Inequality: Because the max(·) function is convex, E[max(T1,…,Tn)] > max(E[T1],…,E[Tn]). Parallelizing tasks pulls completion times deep into the worst-case tail.
The Principal Architect Takeaway: Stop Giving Point Estimates

Whether quoting Recovery Time Objectives (RTO), forecasting cloud FinOps spend, or planning migrations, never provide a single scalar number. Elicit 3-point bounds (a, m, b), run Monte Carlo simulations across your dependency DAG, and present two figures: P50 (Expected Reality) and P90/P95 (Commitment Threshold). Committing to a sum of modes guarantees schedule failure.

5. The Principal Architect's Synthesis

System failures under production load frequently trace back to replacing skewed, stateful distributions with point estimates. Use this diagnostic matrix during architectural design reviews:

Distribution The Naive Assumption The Production Reality Architectural Defense
Geometric
1 / p		 Retries are independent coin flips that improve availability for free. Retries amplify load by 1/p per tier, triggering metastable collapse. Retry Budgets (max 10%), Full Jitter, retry at 1 layer only, Markov Circuit Breakers.
Negative Binomial
σ² = μ + μ²/r		 Arrival counts maintain equidispersion (σ2 = μ). Bursting intensities and scheduled batches generate severe overdispersion (σ2 ≫ μ). Per-record DLQs, tenant velocity controls, Gamma-Poisson queue headroom sizing.
Beta
α / (α + β)		 Zero errors in 50 canary requests indicates a 0% production defect rate. Small samples mask true failure ceilings (3 / n ≈ 6% at 95% confidence). Rule of Three gatekeeping (n ≥ 3/SLA), Thompson Sampling for canary discovery.
Beta-PERT
(a + 4m + b) / 6		 Summing most-likely (m) values accurately projects project or task milestones. Right skew (μ > m) and Jensen's Inequality at DAG merges delay final delivery. 3-point estimation + Monte Carlo DAG simulation; commit to P90, never the Mode.
