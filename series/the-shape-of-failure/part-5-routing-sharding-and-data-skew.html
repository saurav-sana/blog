Routing, Sharding, and the Heavy-Tailed Geometry of Data Skew

October 30, 2026 · Part 5 of 8

“The average does not exist. Look at the variance, or prepare to be liquidated by the tail.”

— Adapted from Nassim Nicholas Taleb, The Black Swan

Every distributed-systems architecture diagram looks magnificent in PowerPoint. Boxes are symmetrically aligned, arrows flow peacefully from left to right, and the word stateless is sprinkled around like holy water to ward off capacity-planning questions.

In the comforting fantasy of Gaussian land, traffic arrives in polite uniform waves, every tenant consumes an average slice of CPU, and deterministic hash partitioning spreads load across shards like warm butter on toast. Then production happens. The toast lands face-down on the datacenter floor, and one whale tenant, one unindexed partition scan, or one synchronized token refresh turns Shard 7 into molten slag while the other 15 shards operate at roughly 75% of their sustainable throughput. The cluster still has spare aggregate capacity, but that capacity is stranded on the wrong shards.

A probability distribution does not cause an outage on its own; it is an empirical description of workload geometry. Outages happen when a heavy-tailed workload collides with a rigid boundary—a deterministic hash ring, a shared FIFO worker pool, or a poorly sized buffer.

Architectural Thesis

Systems fail where workload skew meets a rigid partition boundary. When key popularity, tenant payload cost, or arrival cadence is heavy-tailed, aggregate cluster utilization becomes a vanity metric. You are only as healthy as your hottest shard and your deepest queue.

The Architect's Taxonomy of Skew & Extremes

Hash partitioning makes a bet about key placement: hashing can spread distinct keys across partitions, but it cannot make those keys equally popular or equally expensive. A balanced hash ring is therefore not proof of balanced work. This chapter focuses on two different forms of skew: discrete popularity skew (which keys receive requests) and continuous cost skew (how much work each request or tenant creates). Zipf and Pareto are related power-law families, but they are not interchangeable: Zipf assigns probabilities to discrete ranks; Pareto models a positive continuous magnitude such as bytes or service demand. A Pareto-like size distribution does not follow merely because a workload contains one very large example.

1. Zipf's Law: Hot Keys, Shard Pinning, and Cache Stampedes

In an egalitarian universe, all database keys are created equal. In our Multi-Tenant Analytics Gateway, a single global configuration row or a VIP tenant's auth token mapped through a deterministic partitioning boundary turns a horizontally scaled cluster into a localized smoking crater.

The Mathematical Hypothesis

Zipf's Law models discrete rank-frequency skew. If \( N \) distinct keys are ranked by access frequency (\( k \in \{1, 2, \dots, N\} \)), the probability that a random request targets the key at rank \( k \) is inversely proportional to a power \( s > 0 \) of its rank:

\[ P(K = k) = \frac{k^{-s}}{H_{N,s}}, \quad \text{where } H_{N,s} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} i^{-s} \]

Here \(H_{N,s}\) is the generalized harmonic number. For \(s=1\), \(H_{N,1}\approx\ln N+\gamma\) for large \(N\); for \(s>1\), it converges to \(\zeta(s)\) as \(N\to\infty\). If a service applies a cost that depends on the key, request frequency alone is not enough: measure both request share and resource share.

How much does the top 1% receive? For a finite Zipf population, the share of requests directed to the top \(m=\lceil0.01N\rceil\) keys is \(H_{m,s}/H_{N,s}\). With \(N=10{,}000\) and \(s=1\), this is approximately \(H_{100,1}/H_{10{,}000,1}=5.1874/9.7876\approx53.0\%\). A flatter exponent or a different finite keyspace produces a different share; “top 1% means over half the traffic” is not a universal Zipf property.

Strict Skew Metrics Taxonomy:

Engineers frequently misuse terminology when describing skewed load. We must distinguish:

  • Peak-to-Average (Max-to-Mean) Ratio: \( \frac{\max_i L_i}{\bar{L}} \). Simply how much hotter the worst shard is compared to the average.
  • Coefficient of Variation (CV): \( \frac{\sigma_L}{\mu_L} \). A measure of relative dispersion.
  • Gini Coefficient (\( G \in [0, 1] \)): A strict measure of inequality across all shards: \( G = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} |L_i - L_j|}{2 M^2 \bar{L}} \). Never label a simple peak-to-average ratio as a "Gini coefficient."

Illustrative Engineering Scenario: The Config Key vs. The Sharded Cache

Evidence classification: This is a reproducible hypothetical capacity scenario, not a report of measured production telemetry. Its numbers illustrate how a hot key can overload one shard while aggregate capacity remains available.

1. Observed Problem

The Analytics Gateway relies on a 16-shard Redis cluster for metadata lookups, certified for an aggregate capacity of 24,000 RPS (1,500 RPS per shard). At 10:15 AM, aggregate cluster traffic reaches only 20,000 RPS (83% of cluster capacity), yet upstream Gateway nodes begin throwing massive waves of RedisTimeoutExceptions.

2. Evidence & Telemetry Assumptions (Piecewise Mixture)

The cache contains \(N=10{,}000\) active logical keys. For a Zipf rank-frequency model with \(s=1.0\), the hottest rank has share \(1/H_{10{,}000,1}\approx10.22\%\). To make the routing arithmetic transparent, this is a simplified two-component stress model: retain that Zipf-derived hot-key share, then assume the remaining 89.78% background traffic is split evenly across the 16 shards. The even background split is an explicit scenario assumption, not a consequence guaranteed by hashing or by the Zipf model.

3. Mathematical Model & Derivation

For \( N = 10,000 \) and \( s = 1.0 \), the normalizing constant is:

\[ H_{10,000,1} \approx 9.7876 \]

The single hottest key (\( k = 1 \)) therefore commands a global traffic share of:

\[ P(K = 1) = \frac{1^{-1}}{9.7875} \approx 0.1022 \quad \text{(10.22\% of all cluster traffic)} \]

At an aggregate arrival rate of \( \Lambda = 20,000 \) RPS, key \( k = 1 \) alone generates:

\[ \lambda_{k=1} = 20,000 \times 0.1022 = 2,044 \text{ RPS} \]

The background traffic hitting the cluster minus this hot key is \( 20,000 - 2,044 = 17,956 \) RPS, distributed uniformly as \( 17,956 / 16 = 1,122.25 \) RPS per shard. (Note: 10.22% + 89.78% = 100% of workload accounted for).

4. Failure Mechanism (The Peak-to-Average Ratio)

Key \( k = 1 \) maps deterministically to a specific physical node, let's call it Shard 7. Shard 7 receives all 2,044 RPS from the hot key, plus its 1,122.25 RPS of background traffic:

\[ \lambda_{\text{Shard } 7} \approx 2,044 + 1,122.25 = 3,166.25 \text{ RPS} \]

Shard 7 receives an offered load equal to 2.11 times its 1,500 RPS capacity. This is an overload ratio, not stable utilization: if the shard can serve at most 1,500 RPS, its queue grows while 3,166.25 RPS continue to arrive. Each other shard receives about \(1{,}122.25/1{,}500=74.8\%\) of its nominal capacity. The Peak-to-Average Ratio is:

\[ \frac{\max_i \lambda_i}{\bar{\lambda}} = \frac{3,166.25}{1,250} = 2.53 \]

A low cluster-wide CPU metric effortlessly coexists with a completely saturated shard. Shard 7's connection pool saturates, and any multi-key query touching Shard 7 hangs until timeout.

5. Architectural Controls
  • In-Flight Request Coalescing (singleflight): Deduplicate concurrent cache misses for identical key reads at the proxy tier. However, singleflight only coalesces concurrent requests; it does not eliminate sustained demand after the coalescing window completes.
  • Local Micro-Caches with Jittered TTLs: Cache hot reads in gateway L1 memory for 50–200ms with uniform TTL jitter to prevent synchronized cache stampedes across proxy nodes.
  • Salted Write Sharding: Distributing hot writes introduces ordering problems. Append a random suffix key_1#0 .. key_1#7 to spread hot writes across 8 shards, accepting an 8-way aggregation penalty on reads. Adding cluster shards does NOT split traffic for a single unsalted hot key.

2. Synchronized Token Refresh: Temporal Skew

Popularity skew across keys is spatial. Control-plane systems suffer from a second, equally lethal geometry: temporal arrival skew, where hundreds of thousands of otherwise well-behaved clients phase-lock into a synchronized thundering herd.

[120,000 Gateway Clients] │ Regional network blip heals / Coordinated deployment restart ▼ [Regional API Gateway / Edge] │ Independent Admission Rate Limits & Throttling ▼ [Token Service Fleet] │ Instantaneous 4,000 RPS spike (vs. 133.3 RPS Poisson average) ▼ [HSM / Credential Store] │ Row-lock contention + Cryptographic signing queue exhaustion
1. Illustrative Scenario

An enterprise identity control plane serves \( N = 120,000 \) connected daemon clients. Access tokens have a fixed TTL of \( T = 900 \) seconds (15 minutes). Following a brief 5-second BGP route flap, all 120,000 clients reconnect and request token refreshes within a 30-second window, collapsing the Token Service.

2. Mathematical Model: Poisson Illusion vs. Phase-Locked Reality

During capacity planning, the team modeled token refreshes as a stationary Poisson process:

\[ \bar{\lambda} = \frac{120,000 \text{ clients}}{900 \text{ seconds}} \approx 133.33 \text{ RPS} \]

Provisioning the cluster for 1,500 RPS looked like a conservative 10× safety margin. However, phase-locking compresses all \( N \) arrivals into \( \Delta t = 30 \) seconds:

\[ \lambda_{\text{burst}} = \frac{120,000}{30 \text{ s}} = 4,000 \text{ RPS} \quad \text{(2.67} \times \text{ total cluster capacity)} \]

The Follow-On Avalanche: When clients refresh at a fixed fraction of their token lifetime, synchronized issuance creates synchronized future refresh cohorts. Without randomized TTL jitter, the system repeatedly reproduces a concentrated arrival pattern.

3. Little's Law Admission Control & Damping Math

To break the cohort synchronization and protect downstream systems, consider two separate controls:

  • Admission Rate: Hard limit of 1,200 requests/second at the edge.
  • Concurrency planning (Little's Law): Little's Law (\(L=\lambda W\)) gives average in-system concurrency in a stable system. At an admitted rate of \(1{,}200\) RPS and a mean time in system of \(50\) ms:
    \[ L = 1{,}200\;\text{s}^{-1}\times0.050\;\text{s}=60 \text{ requests on average} \]
    Sixty is expected average occupancy, not a safe hard concurrency ceiling. A configured limit needs headroom for service-time variability and must be validated against downstream capacity, queueing delay, and deadlines.
4. Boundary with Retry Feedback

Randomized refresh scheduling and hierarchical per-tenant admission buckets reduce synchronized arrivals. A delayed retry can simply move a burst into a later window if clients reconverge; retry budgets, backoff, and state-dependent feedback are treated in Part 6 rather than developed here. Persist refresh jitter across restarts so a process restart does not recreate the same cohort.

3. Pareto Distribution: Tenant Skew and HoL Blocking

The "80/20 rule" is not a workload model. Consider a deliberately extreme 99/1 hypothetical stress scenario: 99 tenants each submit a 50 KB incremental query, while one "Whale" tenant submits a 40 GB cross-partition reconciliation query into the same execution pool. This is a two-point workload example—not evidence of a fitted Pareto distribution. It demonstrates why request counts and tenant counts can conceal enormous differences in resource demand.

The Mathematical Hypothesis

A positive continuous random variable \(X\) (for example, an idealized query-byte demand) follows a Type-I Pareto distribution with minimum scale \(x_m>0\) and tail index \(\alpha>0\) when its survival function and density are:

\[ P(X > x) = \left(\frac{x_m}{x}\right)^\alpha, \quad f(x) = \frac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}} \quad \text{for } x \ge x_m \]

For this ideal unbounded model, the moments depend on the tail index:

  • First Moment (Mean): \( \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1} \) for \( \alpha > 1 \). If \( \alpha \le 1 \), the mean is infinite.
  • Second Moment & Variance: \( \text{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha x_m^2}{(\alpha - 1)^2(\alpha - 2)} \) for \( \alpha > 2 \). If \( \alpha \le 2 \), the variance is infinite (\( \sigma^2 \to \infty \)).

Queueing Case Study: Why Service-Time Variability Delays Small Jobs

Evidence classification: The following is an illustrative single-server queueing model with assumed empirical inputs. It is not a claim that a production trace was fitted to an unbounded Pareto distribution.

1. Queueing Scenario

Consider one serialized FIFO worker lane with mean worker occupancy \(E[S_{\mathrm{occ}}]=25\) ms and utilization \(\rho=0.50\). Small lookups can wait behind a long scan even though the lane is busy only half the time on average. This single-server utilization is not the same metric as fleet-wide CPU utilization.

2. Ideal Tail vs. Operational Workload

Suppose scan-byte demand \(B\) resembles an ideal Pareto tail with \(\alpha=1.6\) over the observed range. If service demand were an unbounded affine transform \(S_{\mathrm{latent}}=S_0+B/R_{\mathrm{effective}}\), with a fixed positive effective rate, it would retain that tail index: its mean would be finite but its second moment and variance would be infinite. The ideal unbounded model therefore cannot supply a finite \(C_S^2\) for the ordinary M/G/1 mean-wait calculation.

A real gateway is not that ideal model. Admission limits, maximum scan sizes, execution deadlines, and effective cancellation can bound the operational work a worker actually performs. The resulting workload is bounded or otherwise operationally constrained; its moments are finite when the measured occupancy distribution has a finite second moment. We do not infer this finite distribution merely from \(\alpha=1.6\).

Keep five quantities separate:

  • Latent service requirement \(S_{\mathrm{latent}}\): work the query would demand if it ran to completion without cancellation.
  • Operational worker occupancy \(S_{\mathrm{occ}}\): elapsed time from acquiring the serialized worker slot until the worker releases it, including cancellation and cleanup time. This is the service-time variable used below.
  • Observed occupancy distribution: the empirical distribution of \(S_{\mathrm{occ}}\) for all jobs that occupy the lane, including jobs canceled after starting—not just successfully completed queries.
  • Client-observed latency: queue wait plus execution/cleanup and network or gateway overhead; for canceled requests, the client may observe a deadline or timeout rather than completion latency.
  • Cancellation censoring: when a query is canceled before its latent work finishes, its uncanceled service requirement is right-censored. Completed-query-only samples suffer survivorship bias and cannot be substituted for worker occupancy measurements.

A client timeout bounds worker occupancy only if cancellation reaches the execution engine and the worker actually releases its resources. If cancellation is delayed, non-cooperative, or followed by unbounded cleanup, the timeout is not a service-time cap. If queued jobs abandon before service, the simple M/G/1 model also needs an abandonment extension.

For the numerical illustration, assume a representative empirical sample of actual worker occupancy durations (including started-and-canceled jobs) has \(E[S_{\mathrm{occ}}]=25\) ms and squared coefficient of variation \(C_{S_{\mathrm{occ}}}^2=140\). Treat these as finite empirical inputs, not as moments of the ideal Pareto law. In a real system, stratify by query class and time window, check stationarity and sample representativeness, and quantify uncertainty in the second-moment estimate.

Resource vs. request skew: if 1% of requests each consume 150 times the mean cost of the other 99%, their share of total resource use is \(0.01\times150/(0.01\times150+0.99)\approx60.2\%\). Here 150 is relative to the cost of a background request—not the overall mean including the whale. This is a separate illustrative cost-mixture calculation, not a fit to the 99-tenant/one-whale example above.

3. Pollaczek–Khinchine Calculation

For an M/G/1, FCFS, single-server queue with stationary Poisson arrivals, i.i.d. service times independent of the arrival process, no queue abandonment, and \(\rho=\lambda E[S_{\mathrm{occ}}]<1\), the Pollaczek–Khinchine mean queue-wait formula is:

\[ W_q=\frac{\lambda E[S_{\mathrm{occ}}^2]}{2(1-\rho)}=\frac{\lambda\left(\operatorname{Var}(S_{\mathrm{occ}})+E[S_{\mathrm{occ}}]^2\right)}{2(1-\rho)} \]

Given \(E[S_{\mathrm{occ}}]=0.025\) s and \(\rho=0.50\), the arrival rate implied by utilization is \(\lambda=\rho/E[S]=20\) jobs/s. Since \(C_S^2=\operatorname{Var}(S)/E[S]^2=140\), the second moment is \(E[S^2]=(1+C_S^2)E[S]^2=141(0.025)^2=0.088125\) s\(^2\).

\[ W_q=\frac{20\times0.088125}{2(1-0.50)}=1.7625\text{ s}\approx1{,}763\text{ ms} \]

For deterministic service at the same mean and utilization, \(C_S^2=0\), so \(E[S^2]=(0.025)^2=0.000625\) s\(^2\) and:

\[ W_q=\frac{20\times0.000625}{2(1-0.50)}=0.0125\text{ s}=12.5\text{ ms} \]

Under these assumptions, the empirical high-variability lane produces a mean queue wait about \(141\) times the deterministic-service result at the same mean service time and utilization. This is a queueing-model result, not a universal prediction from the Pareto exponent. The ideal unbounded \(\alpha=1.6\) service model would instead have \(E[S^2]=\infty\), making the ideal M/G/1 mean wait infinite; it cannot be combined with the finite empirical \(C_S^2=140\).

The approximation is deliberately narrow. Bursty or non-Poisson arrivals, tenant-correlated service times, time-varying rates, multiple servers, non-FCFS scheduling, or abandonment can invalidate the M/G/1 estimate. Use the equation to expose the effect of measured service variability, then validate with queue telemetry or a workload-specific simulation.

3. Architectural Controls (SITA-E Workload Isolation)

The M/G/1 example exposes the mechanism: long worker occupancy blocks short jobs in a shared FIFO lane. Reduce the variance presented to each lane and account for resource consumption rather than request count:

  • Fast Lane / Heavy Lane Isolation: Estimate query cost from metadata manifests before execution. Route sub-10MB interactive queries to a Fast Lane and multi-GB analytical scans to a dedicated Heavy Lane worker pool.
  • Resource-weighted fairness: Deficit Round Robin can schedule estimated cost quanta, not merely one turn per request. Charge tenants in measured or estimated units such as CPU-ms, bytes read, I/O operations, and memory byte-seconds; enforce per-tenant concurrency and resource budgets. A request-count quota treats a 50 KB lookup and a 40 GB scan as equal and is therefore not a fairness policy.
  • Deadline-aware cancellation: Cancel running execution graphs when the caller's deadline expires, and verify that workers release CPU, memory, I/O, and concurrency permits. Cancellation reduces operational occupancy only when it is effective.
  • Cost-based admission: Replace naive request-count rate limiters (100 req/min) with per-tenant CPU-millisecond, bytes-read, and memory-byte-second budgets. Estimates need conservative bounds and post-execution reconciliation because manifest estimates can be wrong.

4. Spatial Routing and Combinatorial Isolation

Hash partitioning is rigid. To dynamically route around hot shards and isolate noisy neighbors, distributed systems employ randomized algorithms that explicitly manipulate workload geometry.

Consistent Hashing, Virtual Nodes, and the Hot-Key Limit

Consistent hashing places keys on a ring and assigns each key to its successor node. Adding or removing a node generally remaps only a portion of the keyspace rather than reshuffling every key. Virtual nodes (vnodes) give each physical node multiple ring positions; they smooth placement imbalance, allow heterogeneous node weights, and make incremental rebalancing easier.

Neither a ring nor vnodes divide demand for one logical hot key: all requests for that key still resolve to its owner unless the data model adds another level of indirection. Virtual partitions can be moved, split, or assigned to different workers, but splitting a hot key requires a safe read/write strategy. Replicating a hot read key and distributing reads across replicas can reduce its per-node load; trade-offs include replication lag, cache invalidation, consistency semantics, and extra storage. Hot writes may need salted write partitions plus read-time aggregation or a serialized ordering mechanism.

The Power of Two Random Choices (P2C)

In the classic balls-into-bins result, place \(N\) equal-size jobs sequentially into \(N\) initially empty workers. Uniform random placement gives a maximum load of order \(\log N/\log\log N\) with high probability. If each job independently samples two workers uniformly and chooses the less-loaded one (with ties broken consistently), the maximum number of jobs per worker falls to order \(\log\log N\), up to constant terms.

Assumptions and production boundary: This result assumes comparable unit-size jobs, independent uniform choices, sequential placement, and an accurate view of current load. It is not a universal maximum-query-size or latency guarantee. With heterogeneous queries, queue length can rank a worker incorrectly: one queued 40 GB scan may cost more than many small lookups. Use estimated remaining work or normalized resource pressure (CPU, memory, I/O), account for worker capacity differences, and recognize that stale telemetry can send simultaneous arrivals to the same apparently idle worker.

Combinatorial Shuffle Sharding

In standard shared multi-tenancy, one rogue tenant submitting a "poison-pill" query exhausts workers and cascades failures globally. Shuffle sharding resolves this combinatorially. In a fleet of \( N = 100 \) nodes, assign each tenant a virtual shard of \( k = 4 \) nodes. The probability that two tenants share the exact same 4 nodes is:

\[ P(\text{complete overlap}) = \frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}} = \frac{k!(N-k)!}{N!} = \frac{1}{3,921,225} \]

For independently chosen, uniformly random four-node subsets, the intersection size follows a hypergeometric distribution: \(P(X=j)=\binom{4}{j}\binom{96}{4-j}/\binom{100}{4}\). Exact overlap is rare, but partial overlap is not. Shuffle sharding reduces the number of tenants exposed to one noisy neighbor; it does not guarantee that the other three nodes are unaffected or have spare capacity. Its isolation benefit depends on independent placement, balanced node capacity, and routing that keeps each tenant within its assigned subset.

Cost-Aware Scheduling and Resource-Weighted Fairness

Fairness cannot be measured by request count when requests have radically different costs. A tenant issuing 100 tiny metadata lookups is not equivalent to a tenant issuing 100 multi-gigabyte scans. Track a resource vector per tenant—such as CPU-ms, bytes read, memory byte-seconds, and I/O operations—and enforce weighted budgets and concurrency limits against those dimensions.

For a single schedulable cost unit, Deficit Round Robin can allocate weighted quanta and carry unused deficit forward. For multiple constrained resources, a dominant-resource policy compares each tenant's largest normalized share (usage of a resource divided by that resource's capacity). Neither mechanism is automatically fair if cost estimates are inaccurate or resource dimensions are omitted; reconcile estimates with measured consumption and prevent a tenant from accumulating unbounded concurrent work.

Operational checks: measure per-tenant resource share, queue wait, running and queued concurrency, cancellation effectiveness, and starvation. Test both noisy-neighbor isolation and aggregate utilization; strict isolation can leave capacity idle when a tenant's assigned slice is underused.

5. The Cauchy Regime: Ratio Metrics & Undefined Averages

Every engineer is taught that if you take enough samples (\( N \to \infty \)), the sample mean converges to the true population average. That theorem requires the distribution's absolute first moment to be finite. In Cauchy-like regimes, that condition fails, and your monitoring dashboards hallucinate.

The Mathematical Counterexample

A Cauchy distribution with location peak \( x_0 \) and scale \( \gamma > 0 \) has probability density:

\[ f(x; x_0, \gamma) = \frac{1}{\pi\gamma \left[1 + \left(\frac{x - x_0}{\gamma}\right)^2\right]} \]

The tails decay slowly as \( x^{-2} \). Therefore, the integral defining expected value diverges:

\[ \mathbb{E}[|X|] = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x}{1 + x^2} dx = \infty \]

Operational Relevance: When Averages Lie

API latency itself is strictly nonnegative and bounded by timeouts, so it never literally spans \( -\infty \) to \( +\infty \) like a true Cauchy variable. However, engineers encounter the \( x^{-2} \) Cauchy-like decay in two critical scenarios:

  1. Heavy-Tailed Power Laws exhibiting \( \alpha = 1 \) behavior over an observed range.
  2. Unstable Ratio Metrics: Calculating throughput efficiency \( R = \text{Successful Requests} / \text{Available Capacity} \) during a brownout where the denominator oscillates near zero. This creates enormous, volatile outliers.

By the stability property of the Cauchy distribution, if \(X_i\) are independent and identically distributed \(\operatorname{Cauchy}(x_0,\gamma)\), their sample average has the same distribution:

\[ \bar{X}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i \sim \text{Cauchy}(x_0, \gamma) \]

The Cauchy distribution has no finite population mean or variance, so the usual finite-mean law of large numbers does not apply; the sample average remains Cauchy-distributed at every sample size. The population median is \(x_0\), and its interquartile range is \(2\gamma\). Sample medians and quantiles can still be useful robust summaries, but their uncertainty should be estimated from the data rather than described as universally “rapid.”

The Observability Rule

Never trigger auto-scalers or PagerDuty alerts using avg(ratio) + 3 * stddev(ratio). In heavy-tailed regimes, a single outlier inflates the sample stddev so severely that the threshold becomes unstable and loses statistical meaning. Instead, alert on high-resolution histogram-derived quantiles (\( P_{99}, P_{99.9} \)) combined with the robust Median Absolute Deviation (MAD).
