Reliability, Redundancy, and the Independence Delusion

October 18, 2026 · Part 2 of 8

“Everything fails, all the time.”

— Werner Vogels, CTO of Amazon (usually quoted by architects right before deploying a single point of failure across three availability zones)

Welcome to the mathematics of things breaking. In enterprise architecture reviews, we routinely calculate cluster reliability using the kind of optimistic multiplication usually reserved for lottery tickets. We spin up three database replicas, assume each node has a $99\%$ chance of staying up, multiply $0.01^3$ on a whiteboard, and proudly declare our platform "five-nines highly available." Nobody in the room asks whether all three nodes share the same Top-of-Rack switch, the same kernel security agent, or the same intern running a Terraform script.

This is Replication Theater: counting boxes on an architecture diagram without verifying whether their failure modes are statistically independent. Cloud components share power buses, control planes, IAM token issuers, DNS resolvers, and CI/CD pipelines. Three replicas are useful only to the extent that they fail separately—and that separation must be proven mathematically and enforced via architectural blast doors, not inferred from the fact that you drew three separate rectangles in Lucidchart.

A probability distribution is not a magic incantation that prevents outages; it is a diagnostic model that connects a physical failure mechanism to an engineering control. Below, we examine the three foundational distributions of reliability engineering—Bernoulli, Binomial, and Weibull—and then combine all three to dissect the ultimate modern series-system nightmare: a 16,384-GPU synchronous AI training fleet.

1. The Bernoulli Distribution: Per-Request Trials, Composite Traps, and Self-DDoS

The Intuition: One Coin Flip vs. A Thousand

Ignore your Prometheus cluster dashboard for a moment and look at one single request hitting your gateway. Did it return HTTP 200 ($X=0$), or did it throw a 500/timeout ($X=1$)? That single binary outcome is a Bernoulli trial with failure probability $q$.

The trap isn't the single coin flip—it's what happens when a single user session requires flipping that coin $1,000$ times in a row, and what happens when your SDK tries to "fix" a bad flip by flipping three more coins while the server is on fire.

For a single atomic request, the probability mass function of a Bernoulli random variable $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(q)$ is almost insultingly simple:

$$P(X = k) = q^k (1 - q)^{1 - k}, \quad k \in \{0, 1\}, \quad \mathbb{E}[X] = q$$

Because nobody gets promoted for writing down $\mathbb{E}[X] = q$, engineers quickly move on to setting a service-level objective (say, $99.9\%$ success, so $q = 0.001$) and slapping `@Retryable(maxAttempts = 3)` onto every RPC client. Both decisions look completely reasonable in isolation, and both routinely destroy production systems.

Operational Anchor: AWS S3 Outage (Feb 28, 2017) Read Post-Mortem ↗

What happened: An operator debugging the S3 billing subsystem in us-east-1 executed a playbook command intended to remove a few servers. A typo removed a massive capacity pool supporting the S3 index and placement subsystems. Once capacity dropped below the operational threshold, requests stopped failing independently—and aggressive client retries across the internet turned a capacity dip into a multi-hour regional gridlock.

The Worked Math: Why 99.9% Availability Equals a 63% Job Failure Rate

Epistemic note: The parameters below represent an illustrative architectural model for a data gateway, not internal AWS telemetry.

Suppose you operate a smart data gateway. A single logical user query isn't one request; it is a composite workflow that authenticates a token, fetches table metadata, and reads $M = 1,000$ Parquet footers from object storage. Each sub-request is a Bernoulli trial with failure rate $q = 0.001$ ($99.9\%$ component SLO).

If sub-requests are independent and the logical query requires all $M = 1,000$ reads to succeed, the probability that the user's job fails is:

$$P(\text{Job Failure}) = 1 - (1 - q)^M = 1 - (0.999)^{1000} \approx 0.6323 \text{ (63.23\%)}$$

Read that again: your storage team hits their $99.9\%$ SLA target, collects their quarterly bonus, and goes home happy—while $63.2\%$ of your users' batch queries fail. This is why microservice architectures with deep fan-out trees feel perpetually broken despite every individual service reporting a green dashboard.

To mask this, a well-meaning developer adds client-side retries ($k = 3$ attempts per sub-request). When failures are genuinely independent (a random packet drop), retries are magic: the sub-request failure rate plummets from $q = 10^{-3}$ to $q^3 = 10^{-9}$, rescuing the composite job:

$$P(\text{Job Failure}_{\text{retry}} \mid \text{Independent}) = 1 - \left(1 - 10^{-9}\right)^{1000} \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ (0.0001\%)}$$

The Independence Collapse: Now enters the S3 capacity cliff. When the downstream placement subsystem saturates, trials stop being independent. If your first request times out because the server's thread pool is wedged, retrying 50 milliseconds later does not give you a fresh $0.001$ draw. The conditional probability of failing on attempt 2 given failure on attempt 1 approaches certainty: $P(X_{i,2}=1 \mid X_{i,1}=1) \to 1.0$.

Under state collapse, retries provide zero reliability improvement ($1.0^3 = 1.0$), while multiplying inbound traffic on the dying service by $3\times$:

$$\lambda_{\text{effective}} = \lambda_{\text{base}} \cdot \Big(1 + q + q \cdot P(\text{fail}_2 \mid \text{fail}_1) + \dots \Big) \xrightarrow{P \to 1} 3\lambda_{\text{base}}$$

Your own clients are now executing a coordinated $3\times$ DDoS attack against your storage tier, preventing the index servers from ever recovering.

Architectural Controls Derived from the Math
  • Process-Wide Adaptive Retry Budgets: Ban naive per-request retry loops. Implement a token-bucket retry budget at the client process or Envoy sidecar level: every successful request deposits $0.1$ tokens; every retry withdraws $1.0$ token. As soon as downstream error rates cross $10\%$, the bucket drains to zero and retries halt globally without requiring a central coordinator.
  • Administrative Capacity Blast Doors: Following S3 2017, operational tooling must enforce hard cardinality invariants. Any CLI command or automated scaler attempting to drain more than $X\%$ of an active subsystem pool must hard-fail unless accompanied by a two-party cryptographic lease.
Telemetry Falsification: The Fano Factor Audit

Stop averaging HTTP 5xx rates over 5-minute windows—a $0.1\%$ average hides whether errors arrived as harmless random static or a 30-second total blackout. Compute the Index of Dispersion (Fano Factor) across 1-second buckets: $$D = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{errors}}}{\mu_{\text{errors}}}$$ If $D \approx 1$, errors are independent Poisson arrivals and retries work. If $D \gg 1$, failures are heavily clustered—your independence assumption is dead, and retry budgets must trip immediately.

2. The Binomial Distribution: Quorum Math & The $\beta$-Factor Ceiling

The Architecture Review Delusion

When designing a consensus cluster (etcd, Raft, Paxos), architects reach for the Binomial distribution to answer: "If we run $n=5$ voters and each has a $p=1\%$ failure probability, what are the odds we lose $k \ge 3$ nodes and lose quorum?"

The textbook Binomial equation promises a comforting $0.000985\%$ risk. Unfortunately, the textbook assumes your five servers don't share the same kernel module, configuration push pipeline, or hypervisor bug.

If—and only if—$n$ replicas fail independently with identical probability $p$, the number of failed nodes $X$ follows $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$:

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$$

This formula is responsible for more misplaced confidence in distributed systems than almost any other equation in computer science. Because $p^k$ shrinks exponentially as $k$ grows, it tricks engineers into believing they can buy infinite availability simply by throwing more hardware replicas at a cluster.

Operational Anchor: CrowdStrike Channel File 291 (July 2024) & Knight Capital (2012) Read CrowdStrike RCA ↗

What happened: On July 19, 2024, CrowdStrike pushed a dynamic configuration update (Channel File 291) that bypassed staged rollout rings and landed simultaneously on millions of Windows hosts globally, triggering an out-of-bounds kernel read and instant BSOD regardless of cloud region or availability zone. Twelve years earlier, Knight Capital deployed new trading code across 8 servers; a missed flag on one server and latent legacy code ("Power Peg") across the cluster turned hardware redundancy into a synchronized $\$460\text{M}$ loss in 45 minutes.

The Worked Math: Independent Quorum vs. The Common-Cause Wall

Epistemic note: Illustrative consensus cluster model demonstrating the formal $\beta$-factor common-cause bound.

Consider a 5-voter consensus group ($n = 5$) requiring a majority of 3 nodes to remain available. Each node has an independent hardware/OS unavailability rate of $p_{\text{ind}} = 0.01$ ($99\%$ availability) over an observation window. Under the naive Binomial model, quorum is lost when 3, 4, or all 5 voters fail simultaneously:

$$P(\text{Quorum Loss}_{\text{ind}}) = \sum_{k=3}^{5} \binom{5}{k} (0.01)^k (0.99)^{5-k} = 10(10^{-6})(0.9801) + 5(10^{-8})(0.99) + 10^{-10} \approx 9.85 \times 10^{-6}$$

At $0.000985\%$ (roughly five-nines of quorum availability), everyone congratulates themselves and approves the design doc. Now let’s introduce reality using the Common-Cause $\beta$-Factor Model.

Suppose all 5 nodes subscribe to a shared automated control plane—a kernel security agent, an automated config distributor, a shared TLS certificate rotator, or a single DNS/IAM dependency—that experiences a correlated defect with probability $p_{\text{cc}} = 0.001$ ($0.1\%$) during the same observation window. By the law of total probability:

$$P(\text{Quorum Loss}_{\text{actual}}) = p_{\text{cc}} + (1 - p_{\text{cc}}) \cdot P(X \ge 3 \mid \text{No Common Cause})$$ $$= 0.001 + (0.999)\left(9.85 \times 10^{-6}\right) \approx 0.0010098 \text{ (0.101\%)}$$
Cluster Metric Naive Independent Binomial Coupled Common-Cause Model Architectural Consequence
Single Node Unavailability ($p_{\text{ind}}$) $1.0\%$ $1.0\%$ Identical underlying server hardware
Shared Control-Plane Risk ($p_{\text{cc}}$) $0\%$ (Ignored on whiteboard) $0.1\%$ Shared agent push / config / IAM / cert
5-Node Quorum Loss Probability $0.000985\%$ ($\sim 5$ nines) $0.101\%$ ($\sim 3$ nines) Risk underestimated by $102.5\times$
Scaling Cluster from $n=5$ to $n=7$ Drops to $3.4 \times 10^{-7}$ Stalls at $0.100\%$ Zero availability gain from extra nodes

Look at the bottom row of that table. Once $p_{\text{cc}} \gg p_{\text{ind}}^3$, scaling your cluster from 5 nodes to 7, 9, or 99 nodes buys you zero additional availability. The shared risk term $p_{\text{cc}}$ acts as an impenetrable horizontal ceiling.

Architectural Controls Derived from the Math
  • Cellular Staggered Deployment Waves: Because $p_{\text{cc}}$ dominates, your primary engineering goal is driving $P(\text{All voters receive a mutation simultaneously}) \to 0$. Enforce cellular deployment rings where no two voters in the same quorum belong to the same patch wave. Voter $i$ cannot pull a control-plane update or kernel definition until Voter $i-1$ has baked for $\Delta t$ exceeding your P99 defect detection window.
  • Static Stability of the Data Plane: A running consensus group must survive even if the central control plane, IAM service, or secret manager catches fire. Cache active leases and verify tokens locally via asymmetric public keys so control-plane outages cannot cascade into data-plane quorum loss.
Telemetry Falsification: The Shared-Fate Dependency Matrix

Run an automated daily audit across every consensus group inspecting 6 columns: Availability Zone, Hypervisor Generation, Kernel/Agent Patch Ring, TLS Certificate Expiry Timestamp, DNS Resolver, and CI/CD Wave. Any column where $\ge 3$ out of $5$ voters share an identical value is an unmodeled $p_{\text{cc}}$ term waiting to page you at 3 AM.

3. The Weibull Distribution: Conditional Hazard Rates & Software Aging

The Ticking Time Bomb: Why High Uptime Is Not Proof of Stability

Every engineering org has that one haunted server someone brags about: "That broker has been running for 400 days straight without a restart—don't touch it!"

When failure risk increases with age ($\beta > 1$), surviving for 400 days does not mean the node is battle-tested. It means the node is standing on the edge of a statistical cliff. The Weibull distribution quantifies how both physical hardware and leaky software processes rot over time.

The Exponential distribution assumes systems are memoryless ($\beta = 1$): a 5-year-old hard drive is just as likely to survive the next hour as a brand-new drive. Outside of textbook homework problems, almost nothing works like this. The two-parameter Weibull distribution introduces a shape parameter $\beta$ and scale parameter $\eta$ (characteristic life) to model changing hazard rates over time $t$:

$$S(t) = P(T > t) = e^{-(t/\eta)^\beta}, \qquad h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)} = \frac{\beta}{\eta}\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta - 1}$$
  • $\beta < 1$ (Infant Mortality / Burn-In): Hazard rate decreases over time. Bad solder joints, DOA optical transceivers, and botched config deploys fail immediately; if a node survives the first 24 hours, it stabilizes.
  • $\beta = 1$ (Memoryless Random Failures): Constant hazard rate (reduces to Exponential). Cosmic rays and random network blips.
  • $\beta > 1$ (Wear-Out & Software Aging): Hazard rate accelerates with age. Mechanical bearing wear, SSD NAND oxide degradation, JVM heap fragmentation, uncollected C++/Arrow off-heap buffers, and RocksDB compaction debt.
Operational Anchor: Backblaze Fleet Telemetry & Stateful Worker Exhaustion Explore Backblaze Data ↗

What happens in production: Backblaze’s quarterly reliability reports across $250,000+$ drives show that once drives pass age 3–4 years, annualized failure rates spike non-linearly as mechanical wear-out ($\beta > 1$) takes over. In software infrastructure, homogeneous clusters deployed simultaneously hit memory fragmentation or descriptor exhaustion cliffs in lockstep.

The Worked Math: Cumulative Failure vs. Conditional Residual Risk

Epistemic note: Illustrative Weibull parameters ($\beta=3, \eta=5$) modeling an aging storage cohort or stateful worker pool; raw Backblaze AFR tables require Kaplan-Meier fitting with right-censoring before extracting $\beta$.

Suppose your stateful storage nodes follow a Weibull lifetime distribution with shape $\beta = 3.0$ (accelerating wear-out) and scale $\eta = 5.0\text{ years}$ (or, for a leaky software worker, $\eta = 50\text{ days}$ of uptime). The cumulative probability that a node fails by age $t = 4$ is:

$$F(4) = 1 - S(4) = 1 - e^{-(4/5)^3} = 1 - e^{-0.512} \approx 39.95\%$$

Here is where naive capacity planning goes off the rails. As an operator looking at the nodes currently running in production at age $t_0 = 4$, the cumulative number $F(4)$ is history. You need the Conditional Probability of Failure over the next planning window ($\Delta t = 1$) given that the node has already survived to age 4:

$$P(4 < T \le 5 \mid T > 4) = 1 - \frac{S(5)}{S(4)} = 1 - \exp\left[\left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^3 - \left(\frac{5}{5}\right)^3\right] = 1 - e^{-0.488} \approx \mathbf{38.63\%}$$

Compare that against a fresh node during its very first window ($t = 0 \to 1$):

$$P(0 < T \le 1 \mid T > 0) = 1 - e^{-(1/5)^3} = 1 - e^{-0.008} \approx \mathbf{0.80\%}$$

A node that has "proven itself" by surviving for 4 intervals is $48\times$ more likely to crash in the upcoming window than a fresh node ($38.63\%$ vs. $0.80\%$). If you provisioned all your nodes on the same day, $38.6\%$ of your surviving fleet is going to detonate during window 5, overwhelming your erasure-coding or replica rebuild bandwidth.

Architectural Controls Derived from the Math
  • Jittered Proactive Rejuvenation: Never let stateful software workers or hardware cohorts age in lockstep. Trigger automated rolling drains and restarts at age $t_{\text{recycle}} \pm \text{Uniform}(-20\%, +20\%)$, chosen where the conditional hazard $h(t)$ crosses your background replica-rebuild bandwidth limit.
  • Separate Deterministic Leaks from Stochastic Aging: Do not fit a Weibull curve to a deterministic memory leak. If $\text{d(RSS)}/\text{d}t$ is a straight line per 1,000 requests, fix the pointer bug or alert on memory slope. Reserve Weibull modeling for compound wear-out where failure is a stochastic function of varied multi-tenant workloads.
Telemetry Falsification: Right-Censoring Discipline (Kaplan-Meier)

The most common statistical blunder in postmortems is calculating MTBF by averaging the uptime of nodes that crashed—completely ignoring the 900 healthy nodes still running or gracefully terminated by deployments. That is textbook survivorship bias. Always estimate $(\beta, \eta)$ using a Kaplan-Meier survival estimator or Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) that treats every healthy running node and graceful deploy restart as a right-censored observation ($T > t_{\text{observed}}$).

4. Capstone Synthesis: The 16,384-GPU Synchronous Trap & Goodput Economics

Where All Three Distributions Collide: AI Infrastructure at Scale

In stateless web services, $N$ nodes provide parallel redundancy. In synchronous LLM pre-training (Ring-AllReduce / FSDP across NVLink and InfiniBand), $N = 16,384$ GPUs are wired in series.

If one HBM3 memory bank throws an uncorrectable ECC error or one optical transceiver flaps, all 16,384 GPUs stall immediately. At this scale, reliability math stops being an academic exercise and directly governs whether you burn $\$16\text{ Million}$ in wasted GPU compute.

Operational Anchor: Meta Llama 3.1 (405B) Training Cluster (2024) Read Llama 3 Paper (Sec 3) ↗

The documented numbers: During a 54-day pre-training snapshot of Llama 3 405B across $N = 16,384$ Nvidia H100 (80GB) GPUs, Meta recorded $419$ unexpected job interruptions ($148$ faulty GPUs, $72$ HBM3 memory errors, $19$ SRAM faults, plus switch and cable flaps)—averaging one cluster-halting failure every $3.09\text{ hours}$.

Step A: Series Fragility — How 99.95% Reliable GPUs Crash Every 3 Hours

Epistemic note: Cluster size ($16,384$) and interruption count ($419$ over $54$ days) are empirical figures from Meta's Llama 3 report; cost rates, checkpoint stall ($\delta$), and restart times ($R$) are illustrative FinOps planning models.

Over 54 days ($1,296\text{ hours}$), $16,384$ GPUs accumulate $21,233,664\text{ GPU-hours}$ of runtime. Dividing $419$ interruptions by total GPU-hours yields the individual per-GPU failure rate $\lambda_{\text{gpu}}$:

$$\lambda_{\text{gpu}} = \frac{419}{21,233,664\text{ GPU-hours}} \approx 1.973 \times 10^{-5}\text{ failures / GPU-hour}$$

In isolation, that is phenomenal hardware engineering: a single H100 has an MTBF of $50,677\text{ hours}$ ($\sim 5.78\text{ years}$), or a $99.95\%$ chance of surviving a 24-hour day. If you ran a small 8-GPU box, you would see a hardware crash once every 264 days.

But in synchronous training, all $N = 16,384$ GPUs must complete every step barrier together. Because the system is wired in series, the cluster failure rate scales linearly with $N$ ($\Lambda_{\text{cluster}} = N \cdot \lambda_{\text{gpu}}$):

$$M_{\text{cluster}} = \text{Cluster MTBF} = \frac{1}{N \cdot \lambda_{\text{gpu}}} = \frac{50,677\text{ hours}}{16,384} \approx \mathbf{3.09\text{ hours (185.4 minutes)}}$$

The probability that your $16,384$-GPU job runs for a single 24-hour day without crashing is $P(T > 24\text{h}) = e^{-24 / 3.09} \approx \mathbf{0.042\%}$. Outages are no longer "incidents"—they are your steady-state operating environment.

Step B: The Young-Daly Equation — Turning Failure Math into $\$13.6\text{M}$ of Saved Compute

Because the cluster crashes every $M = 185.4\text{ minutes}$, you must periodically pause training to write model checkpoints to storage. This creates a classic economic trade-off:

  • Checkpoint too frequently (small $\tau$): You waste massive GPU time stalled on storage I/O ($\delta$ minutes per checkpoint).
  • Checkpoint too rarely (large $\tau$): When a crash occurs, you lose an average of $\frac{\tau}{2}$ minutes of computed gradients, plus $R$ minutes of restart overhead (detecting the hung NCCL collective, draining the dead node, re-initializing $16,384$ ranks, and pulling weights from storage).

The fraction of total fleet wall-clock time wasted—$\text{Waste}(\tau)$—is the sum of checkpoint write tax and crash rollback/recovery tax:

$$\text{Waste}(\tau) = \underbrace{\frac{\delta}{\tau}}_{\text{Checkpoint Write Tax}} + \underbrace{\frac{\frac{\tau}{2} + R}{M}}_{\text{Rollback + Restart Tax}}$$

Taking the derivative with respect to $\tau$ and setting $\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\tau}\text{Waste}(\tau) = -\frac{\delta}{\tau^2} + \frac{1}{2M} = 0$ gives Young’s First-Order Optimal Checkpoint Interval ($\tau^*$):

$$\tau^* = \sqrt{2 \cdot \delta \cdot M}$$

Assume an amortized cluster cost of $\$2.50\text{ per GPU-hour}$ ($\$40,960/\text{hour}$ for the fleet, or $\$53.08\text{ Million}$ over 54 days). For a synchronous blocking checkpoint stall of $\delta = 2.0\text{ min}$ and cold restart time $R = 20.0\text{ min}$, the optimal interval is $\tau^* = \sqrt{2 \times 2.0 \times 185.4} \approx \mathbf{27.2\text{ minutes}}$.

Step C: Sizing the Warm-Spare Buffer with Weibull ($\beta < 1$ vs. $\beta > 1$)

Look at Row 3: how do you slash restart time $R$ from $20\text{ minutes}$ down to $4\text{ minutes}$? You cannot wait for cloud provisioning or hardware technicians when a GPU dies; you must immediately swap in an pre-initialized Warm-Spare Node while the broken node enters a 24-hour repair/burn-in loop ($T_{\text{repair}} = 24\text{h}$).

With a cluster failure occurring every $3.09\text{ hours}$, the average number of broken nodes in the 24-hour repair pipeline is:

$$\mu_{\text{repair}} = \Lambda_{\text{cluster}} \cdot T_{\text{repair}} = \frac{24\text{ hours}}{3.09\text{ hours}} \approx 7.77\text{ nodes/day}$$

To guarantee a $99.9\%$ probability that your warm-spare pool never runs dry during a bad day of failures (which would spike $R$ back to $45+$ minutes), we solve for the Poisson tail $P(X > S \mid \mu = 7.77) < 0.001$, which requires $S = 18\text{ warm-spare nodes}$ ($144\text{ GPUs}$, or just $0.88\%$ fleet overhead). Paying a $0.88\%$ CapEx tax for 18 idle warm spares rescues $21\%$ of cluster Goodput across the other $16,384$ GPUs.

Architectural Controls Derived from the Math
  • Two-Tier Asynchronous Checkpointing (Crushing $\delta$): Never block GPU tensor cores on remote object-store I/O. Copy model shards over PCIe into local host DRAM / NVMe in $\delta = 0.2\text{ min}$ ($12\text{ seconds}$), immediately resume training step $t+1$, and flush from host RAM to distributed storage asynchronously in the background.
  • Weibull Infant-Mortality Quarantine Gate ($\beta < 1$ Control): Freshly racked or repaired GPU nodes exhibit steep infant mortality ($\beta \approx 0.6$) due to loose OSFP optics and marginal HBM3 solder joints. Never admit a new or repaired node directly into the 16K synchronous ring; force it through a 24-hour high-TDP burn-in gauntlet (synthetic GEMM + NCCL all-reduce stress) until its conditional hazard rate drops to the fleet baseline.
  • Out-of-Band Flight Recorder (Crushing $R$): When Rank 4,112 hangs on a degraded optical transceiver, all $16,384$ ranks throw an identical NCCL collective timeout. If you wait for the default 10-minute PyTorch timeout and restart blindly, your scheduler will place the job right back onto the broken node ($P(\text{Fail}_2 \mid \text{Fail}_1) = 1.0$). Run a $100\text{ms}$ out-of-band daemon scraping PCIe AER errors, NVLink CRC retries, and NIC link flaps to cordon the exact culprit node within 15 seconds.
Telemetry Falsification: Effective Goodput vs. Node Uptime

Ban "Node Uptime %" as a primary KPI for AI infrastructure. A single thermally throttling GPU running at $70\%$ clock speed forces all $16,383$ healthy GPUs to idle at every AllReduce barrier while reporting $100\%$ uptime. Measure Effective Goodput: the ratio of productive FLOPs permanently committed to storage checkpoints divided by total wall-clock cluster FLOPs.
