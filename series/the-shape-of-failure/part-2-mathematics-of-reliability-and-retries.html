Reliability, Redundancy, and the Independence Delusion

October 18, 2026 · Part 2 of 8

“Everything fails, all the time.”

— Werner Vogels, CTO of Amazon

Welcome to the mathematics of things breaking. In systems design, we routinely calculate cluster reliability using simple probabilities. We spin up three database replicas, calculate the probability of one dying, and proudly declare our architecture "highly available" because we assume that if Server A dies, Server B is completely unaffected.

This assumption is what we call Replication Theater. True independence in the cloud is a myth. Components assumed to be mathematically independent turn out to share a rack, a power domain, a Top-of-Rack switch, a hypervisor, a control plane, or a deployment pipeline. You don't have three independent servers; you have three tenants in the same burning building.

To accurately model failures, we must map our architecture to the correct statistical distribution. Applying the wrong mathematical model to an infrastructure failure is how you end up paging on-call engineers at 3 AM for a phantom metric.

1. The Bernoulli Distribution

The Intuition: The Binary Reality

Forget the cluster. Forget the fleet. Forget the 99.99% SLA dashboard. Think about one single request happening right now. Did the HTTP request succeed, or did it return a 500? Did the server boot, or did it hang?

Everything in your complex distributed system eventually bottoms out here: a single binary event. A user clicks "buy," and either the transaction goes through or it doesn't. There is no "partially degraded" in a strict Bernoulli model. It is a single coin flip: Success (1) or Fail (0).

When an S3 load balancer drops your specific packet, you are a 0. The user doesn't care that the fleet is healthy; their specific coin flip just landed on tails.

The Math

The probability mass function for a Bernoulli trial, where p is the probability of success, and k is the outcome (0 for failure, 1 for success):

P(X = k) = pk(1 - p)1 - k
Let's decode this:
  • If the request succeeds (k = 1): The formula becomes p^1 * (1-p)^0, which is just p.
  • If the request fails (k = 0): The formula becomes p^0 * (1-p)^1, which is just 1 - p.

Worked Example: If your single server has a 99% success rate (p = 0.99), the probability of a successful request is 0.99^1 = 0.99. The probability of failure is 1 - 0.99 = 0.01 (or 1%).

The SRE Trap

The Bernoulli distribution tells you the probability of one event. It tells you absolutely nothing about the system as a whole. If you average out 1,000,000 requests and get a 99.9% success rate, you are masking the fact that 1,000 users just experienced a hard 0.

Every 10 seconds, the Kubelet asks the pod "Are you alive?" The pod's response is a Bernoulli trial. If it returns three 0s in a row, the pod is killed. The Kubelet doesn't care if the pod is "a little bit tired."

2. The Binomial Distribution

The Intuition: The Architecture Review Lie

If a Bernoulli trial is one server flipping a coin, the Binomial distribution is 100 servers flipping coins at the exact same time. It models the probability of exactly k simultaneous failures in a cluster.

This is the math your cloud architect used when they said, "With 5 replicas, the chance of losing quorum is statistically zero!" It assumes every server is perfectly independent.

The fatal flaw: The architecture diagram says "3 AZs, 3 independent replicas." The physical reality is one Top-of-Rack switch losing power, causing 20 servers to land on tails at the exact same millisecond. They aren't independent coins; they are coins glued together.

The Math

The probability of observing exactly k failures out of n total nodes, where each node has a probability p of failing:

P(X = k) = (n choose k) · pk · (1 - p)n - k
Let's decode this:
  • (n choose k): This is the combinatorics part. It calculates the number of different ways k nodes can fail out of n total nodes. (e.g., If node 1 and 2 fail, or node 2 and 3 fail).
  • pk: The probability that exactly k nodes fail.
  • (1 - p)n - k: The probability that the remaining n - k nodes survive.

Worked Example: You have a 5-node database cluster (n = 5). Each node has a 1% chance of dying (p = 0.01). What is the probability that exactly 2 nodes fail? (Which would cause you to lose quorum). The math says 0.097%. Looks safe, right? But this math assumes the failures are independent.

The SRE Trap

The Binomial distribution is dangerously optimistic. It assumes failures are independent coin flips. But in the real world, failures are highly correlated. If you deploy a bad kernel update, p doesn't stay at 1%; it jumps to 100% for every single node.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Correlated Poison" CrowdStrike 2024

You have 100,000 Windows servers. You assume the chance of all failing is mathematically zero. But they didn't flip independent coins; they all downloaded the exact same kernel-level update payload. The probability wasn't independent; it was p = 1 for everyone who received the update. The Binomial assumption shattered.

Reference: CrowdStrike Channel File 291 Root Cause Analysis
The "Quorum Illusion" Raft / Paxos

You have a 5-node cluster. You assume 2 can die safely. But if the shared Top-of-Rack switch dies, all 5 nodes lose network connection simultaneously. The quorum is gone instantly. The Binomial formula assumes the failures are independent, but physical infrastructure is a shared single point of failure.

Reference: Ongaro & Ousterhout, "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm" (Raft Paper)
The "Self-Inflicted DDoS" Redis Cache Stampede

A popular cache key expires. 10,000 clients simultaneously miss the cache and slam the database at the same millisecond. The database drops 80% of the queries. It wasn't random load; it was highly correlated timing.

Reference: Facebook, "Scaling Memcache at Facebook" (NSDI '13)

3. The Weibull Distribution

The Intuition: The Ticking Time Bomb

Hardware doesn't flip coins. Hardware gets tired and dies. The Weibull distribution models the hazard rate over time. It creates the infamous "Bathtub Curve," which has three distinct phases:

  • Infant Mortality (Early Life): High failure rate right out of the box. (Think: a bad batch of hard drives, or a botched Kubernetes deployment).
  • Useful Life (Middle): A flat, random failure rate. (Think: standard operating conditions where failures are purely random).
  • Wear-Out (Late Life): A terrifying spike in failures as physical components degrade. (Think: mechanical drives hitting their spin limit, or a memory leak finally hitting OOM).

If you use Bernoulli for hardware, you'll budget for the flat middle and get absolutely destroyed by the cliff at the end.

The Math

The probability density function for a Weibull distribution, where k is the shape parameter (determines the bathtub curve shape) and λ is the scale parameter (the characteristic life):

f(x; λ, k) = (k / λ) · (x / λ)k - 1 · e-(x/λ)k
Let's decode this:
  • k (Shape): This is the magic number.
    • If k < 1, the failure rate decreases over time (Infant Mortality).
    • If k = 1, the failure rate is constant (Random Failures).
    • If k > 1, the failure rate increases over time (Wear-Out).
  • λ (Scale): The characteristic life. It's the point in time where exactly 63.2% of the components have failed.

Worked Example: If you model an SSD, you might find k = 3 and λ = 5 years. This means the drive works fine for a few years, but as it approaches year 5, the hazard rate spikes exponentially. If you used a constant failure rate (k=1), you would drastically underestimate the risk of failure in year 6.

The SRE Trap

The Weibull distribution is a double-edged sword. It tells you exactly when your hardware will die, but it requires you to have historical data to calculate k and λ. If you just guess, your reliability model is fiction. You need to actively monitor hardware age and usage.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Bathtub" in the Data Center Backblaze HDD Stats

Backblaze publishes their drive failure stats. They don't fail randomly. You see the Weibull curve in action: bad batches fail immediately out of the box (infant mortality). Then, years of quiet stability. Then, at year 4 or 5, the failure rate spikes violently as the mechanical parts wear out.

Reference: Backblaze Quarterly Hard Drive Stats
The "Silent Killer" SSD / EBS Wear-Out

SSDs don't slow down gracefully to 0 IOPS. They work perfectly until they hit their NAND write limit, and then abruptly turn into a brick. The Weibull shape parameter k > 1 models this terrifying cliff-edge hazard rate.

Reference: Meza et al., "A Large-Scale Study of Flash Memory Failures in the Field"
The "Slow Death" OOM Kill

A service with a slow memory leak. The probability of an OOM kill doesn't follow a coin flip. The hazard rate increases every hour the pod stays alive, until it reaches the absolute physical limit of RAM. This is late-stage Weibull wear-out applied to software.

Reference: Linux Kernel OOM Killer Documentation
The Core Problem: Knight Capital (2012)

Knight Capital lost $460 million in 45 minutes not because of a "heavy-tailed statistical anomaly" or a stochastic hardware blip. They collapsed because a deterministic human left an obsolete code path active on one of eight routing servers during a manual deployment. N-way redundancy cannot save you from deterministic state corruption.

The Lesson: Redundancy without independence provides absolutely zero protection. Throwing more nodes at a problem only works if the nodes fail randomly. If the failure is baked into the procedural pipeline, scaling up just means you scale the blast radius.
