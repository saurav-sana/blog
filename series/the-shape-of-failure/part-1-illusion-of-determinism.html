The Shape of Failure · Part 1 of 8

The Illusion of Determinism

Model uncertainty before turning reliability targets into architecture and FinOps decisions

October 17, 2026 · Probability, Queues & Distributed Systems

“If you measure latency using an average, you are measuring the average temperature of the hospital to see if an individual patient is dead.”

— Gil Tene, How NOT to Measure Latency

Every distributed system is, underneath the YAML, RPC stubs, dashboards, and suspiciously clean architecture diagrams, a machine operating under uncertainty.

Requests arrive unevenly. Payloads vary by orders of magnitude. Queues accumulate silently between polling intervals. Downstream dependencies stall. Hardware fails unevenly. Retries synchronize into destructive pulses. Tenants exhibit radically different traffic profiles. Components assumed to be independent on a whiteboard turn out to share a rack, power domain, top-of-rack switch, hypervisor, control plane, DNS resolver, identity service, or deployment pipeline.

The difficult problem in distributed systems is not merely failure; it is the shape of failure.

Two systems can exhibit identical average latency, identical average CPU utilization, and identical nominal availability while possessing radically different probabilities of catastrophic collapse. One system exhibits thin, predictable tails. The other behaves nominally for weeks, then produces an exceedance event large enough to deplete an entire quarterly error budget in six minutes. The averages are identical; the engineering problems are not.

Good distributed-system design makes uncertainty explicit, measures the assumptions behind it, and creates architectural failure boundaries that remain safe when those assumptions fail.

Recurring Architecture Spine & Epistemic Honesty

To keep this guide grounded in real systems rather than textbook trivia, we trace one recurring production architecture across every section: a Multi-Tenant Analytics Gateway (stateless DuckDB + Apache Arrow query workers reading Apache Iceberg tables over object storage, with Redis metadata caching and multi-AZ routing).

A note on evidence: Throughout this article, we explicitly distinguish between Documented Industry Evidence (published empirical findings from Google SRE, AWS, and systems literature) and Illustrative Engineering Calculations (reproducible mathematical scenarios showing how to compute parameters, validate hypotheses, and size controls under explicitly stated assumptions).

1. The Intellectual Spine & The Modeling Workflow

The Central Thesis

Do not ask only: "What is the average?"

Ask: "What stochastic process produced this observation? What exact random variable am I measuring? What assumptions does my model make? What empirical evidence falsifies those assumptions? And what architectural decision changes if those assumptions are wrong?"

The Engineering Workflow
Observed Phenomenon → Data-Generating Process → Random Variable → Assumptions → Candidate Model → Validation & Falsification → Operational Control

This is the exact opposite of statistical cargo-culting:

The Anti-Pattern
Incident ⇏ Textbook Distribution ⇏ Prescribed Architecture

A candidate distribution can fit historical telemetry within tight error bounds during stable operating regimes, only to collapse under failure conditions because deployment topologies, retry loops, or network routing altered the underlying data-generating mechanism.

An architect evaluates any model against five operational criteria:

  1. Mechanistically Plausible: It reflects the physical execution path of the runtime.
  2. Empirically Defensible: Production telemetry does not contradict its core quantiles (\(p95\), \(p99\), \(p99.9\)) on out-of-sample windows.
  3. Decision-Relevant: It estimates the quantity that directly drives an architectural choice (timeout, buffer size, worker concurrency, or replica count).
  4. Robust to Model Error: The system degrades gracefully when reality deviates from the fitted curve.
  5. Continuously Monitorable: Telemetry agents can detect when the workload transitions across statistical regimes.

The First Rule: A Distribution Is a Model, Not a Fact

A production metrics collector records raw timestamps, counters, and durations. It does not record labels stating THIS REQUEST = LOG-NORMAL or THIS FAILURE = POISSON.

Those labels are hypotheses imposed by engineers. Production systems are mixtures of deterministic state machines, stochastic traffic variation, temporal dependencies, common-cause failure couplings, non-linear feedback loops, workload regime changes, and hard physical limits.

The operational question is never: "Which distribution does this metric follow?" It is: "Which model is sufficiently accurate for the decision I need to make?"

2. Four Places Where Textbook Models Collide with Production

Below are the four classic scenarios where unvalidated textbook models collide with production physics—paired with reproducible calculations and concrete engineering controls.

1. Execution Timeouts vs. Log-Normal & Weibull Distributions

Worked Scenario A · Gateway timeout sizing & censoring Illustrative calculation + Google SRE grounding

The Core Trap: Fitting One Curve to Multiple Realities

When setting a timeout (e.g., how long our query gateway waits before dropping a downstream worker call), engineers often try to fit empirical latency to a single continuous probability curve, like a Log-Normal or Weibull distribution.

Neither distribution is inherently invalid for latency within a single regime. The trap is that continuous unimodal curves assume a single smooth decay, whereas real distributed architectures execute across finite mixtures of distinct paths. In this scenario's workload profile:

  • Path A (85% of traffic): Hits local Arrow memory or Redis cache and completes in ~18 ms.
  • Path B (15% of traffic): Misses the cache, requiring cold object-store reads (S3/Iceberg manifests), metadata locks, or hitting GC pauses, taking 100–300 ms.

When you force a single unimodal curve over both populations, the volume of fast 18 ms cache hits drags the fitted mean and variance down, smoothing over the multimodal "cliff" where cold storage queries execute and yielding an artificially tight, dangerous timeout estimate.

Random Variable & Empirical Setup

Let \(X\) be downstream query latency (ms) measured from request dispatch to last response byte. Suppose 1,000,000 representative requests yield these empirical quantiles versus an unsegmented Log-Normal fit:

Metric Measured HDR Histogram Unsegmented Log-Normal Fit Operational Interpretation
p50 (median) 18 ms 19 ms Accurate because the median is dominated by the 85% hot cache-hit population.
p95 95 ms 68 ms Unimodal fit underestimates the transition to cold S3 reads.
p99 180 ms 112 ms Multi-partition object-store reads + metadata lock contention kick in; curve is blind to it.
p99.9 280 ms 190 ms Tail regime; naive fit misses empirical p99.9 by 32%, killing healthy cold queries if used as a timeout.
$$\text{Exceedances above 280 ms (p99.9)} = 1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.001 = 1{,}000 \text{ requests per million}$$

The Timeout Censoring & Late-Completion Trap

Setting a downstream timeout exactly at the p99.9 mark of 280 ms cuts off roughly 1,000 requests per million. However, exceeding a threshold is not identical to structural failure. Two severe failure modes follow:

  • Statistical Censoring & Obscured Latency: Terminating those 1,000 requests does not mean they were deadlocked—many were legitimate queries that would have completed successfully at 295 ms. Telemetry merely records them as "280 ms (Timed Out)," permanently censoring the true tail distribution.
  • Zombie Work & Load Amplification: If the gateway times out the caller socket without cancelling the backend worker, immediate client retries create up to 1,000 additional concurrent queries while the original 1,000 jobs continue consuming CPU and memory in the background.

Engineering Controls

  • Population separation: Never lump all traffic into one latency histogram. Segment telemetry by cache_state (hit vs. miss), tenant_tier, and query_class before evaluating tail quantiles.
  • Deadline & cancellation propagation: Propagate remaining caller deadlines (grpc-timeout / X-Deadline-Ms) down the stack. Wire socket close events directly to duckdb_interrupt() so abandoned queries immediately abort.
  • Instrumentation of censored outcomes: Specifically instrument timeout_censored_requests, socket cancellation acknowledgments, continuing background worker threads, and late successful completions. This allows engineering to determine whether a timeout is protecting capacity or merely hiding unfinished work.

The solution. Base operational timeouts on stratified empirical percentiles rather than unimodal curve fits. Pair caller timeouts with end-to-end cancellation propagation and monitor late completions to ensure abandoned work does not consume capacity.

2. Rare Exceedances vs. Extreme Value Theory (EVT)

Worked Scenario B · Sizing burst headroom with the Generalized Pareto Illustrative calculation + NIST statistical reference

The Core Trap: Using Center-Fitted Math to Predict Tail Disasters

A convenient distribution fitted to the center of observed telemetry (such as a Gaussian or standard Poisson model) severely underestimates rare extremes when tail assumptions do not match reality. Gaussian tails decay exponentially fast ($e^{-x^2}$), whereas distributed systems subject to resource contention exhibit heavy power-law tails. Extrapolating a center-fitted model into high-consequence tails creates dangerous blind spots.

Assumed Scenario Parameters & Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) Setup

Suppose our gateway experiences periodic latency spikes during peak reporting windows. We apply a Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) formulation above a high threshold \(u = 500\,\text{ms}\), where empirical telemetry indicates 1% (\(0.01\)) of all requests exceed \(u\):

  • Exceedance variable (\(Y\)): For any request \(X\) exceeding \(u = 500\,\text{ms}\), let \(Y = X - 500\) represent the excess duration.
  • Assumed fitted GPD parameters: For this illustrative scenario, we assume a fitted Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) with scale parameter \(\beta = 100\,\text{ms}\) and shape parameter \(\xi = 0.2\). Because \(\xi > 0\), the tail exhibits heavy power-law decay. (In production, these parameters must be derived using parameter stability plots and profile likelihood confidence intervals across candidate thresholds.)

The conditional survival function for exceeding an additional duration \(y\) beyond \(u\) is:

$$P(Y > y \mid X > u) = \left(1 + \frac{\xi\,y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi}$$

To calculate the probability of a request exceeding 1 second (\(X > 1000\,\text{ms}\), meaning an excess of \(y = 1000 - 500 = 500\,\text{ms}\)):

$$\begin{aligned} P(X > 1000 \mid X > 500) &= \left(1 + \frac{0.2 \times 500}{100}\right)^{-1/0.2} = (1 + 1)^{-5} = 2^{-5} = 0.03125 \;(3.125\%) \\[6pt] P(X > 1000) &= P(X > 500) \times P(X > 1000 \mid X > 500) = 0.01 \times 0.03125 = 0.0003125 \;(\approx 313 \text{ per million requests}) \end{aligned}$$

Engineering Controls

  • De-clustering & threshold stability: Correlated infrastructure stalls (such as a shared SSD pause or GC pause) cause concurrent queries to spike together. De-cluster temporally correlated bursts into distinct exceedance events, verify parameter stability across \(u \in [400, 650]\,\text{ms}\), and validate predictions against held-out stress windows.
  • Bulkhead lanes & admission caps: Use EVT return-level estimates to size isolated heavy-query bulkhead lanes, calibrate load-shedding admission controllers, and configure memory admission semaphores so the ~313 monster queries per million cannot exhaust memory pools.

The solution. When modeling rare events far out in the tail, use Extreme Value Theory (EVT) rather than center-fitted curves. Verify threshold stability, de-cluster correlated bursts, and account for parameter estimation error before sizing capacity reserves.

3. Queue Collapse vs. Modeling Arrivals & Utilization

Worked Scenario C · The utilization cliff in a single-server queue Illustrative calculation + queueing physics

The Core Trap: Confusing Service Time with Total Response Time

When sizing worker pools or commit engines, knowing the isolated service-time distribution (how fast a worker processes a job, \(S\)) is insufficient. Requests arrive stochastically; random bunches form queues. As utilization \(\rho\) approaches 100%, idle gaps vanish, and waiting time explodes nonlinearly.

Kingman’s Approximation & Single-Server Nuance

Consider a serialized commit queue where mean service time is \(\mathbb{E}[S] = 10\,\text{ms}\) (maximum capacity \(\mu = 100\,\text{req/s}\)). Assume arrivals are approximately Poisson (squared coefficient of variation \(c_a^2 = 1\)) and service-time variability is \(c_s^2 = 1\). Under Kingman’s GI/G/1 heavy-traffic approximation, the mean queue wait time (\(W_q\)) is:

$$W_q \approx \left(\frac{c_a^2 + c_s^2}{2}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\right) \cdot \mathbb{E}[S]$$
Utilization \(\rho\) Arrival Rate \(\lambda\) Multiplier \(\left(\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\right)\) Mean Queue Wait \(W_q\) Total Time \(W = W_q + \mathbb{E}[S]\) Mean In-Flight Work \(L = \lambda W\)
50% 50 req/s \(1\times\) 10 ms 20 ms 1.0 request
80% 80 req/s \(4\times\) 40 ms 50 ms 4.0 requests
90% 90 req/s \(9\times\) 90 ms 100 ms 9.0 requests
95% 95 req/s \(19\times\) 190 ms (\(19 \times \mathbb{E}[S]\)) 200 ms 19.0 requests

Scope & modeling limits: At 95% utilization, the server executes the query in 10 ms, but the caller waits 200 ms. Note that Kingman's formula approximates mean queue wait in a single-server queue; it does not predict tail quantiles (\(p99\)), nor does it directly model a multi-server worker pool. For production worker pools, validate with multi-server GI/G/c models or trace-driven discrete-event simulation.

Engineering Controls

  • Bounded queues & early shedding: Enforce hard queue length limits. Reject (HTTP 429) or shed load immediately at the gateway when expected queue wait exceeds caller deadline budgets.
  • Disaggregated instrumentation: Record queue_wait_ms separately from service_exec_ms. Reconcile concurrency, throughput, and latency using Little’s Law (\(L = \lambda W\)) to detect whether latency degradation is caused by slow execution or queue queuing.
  • Per-tenant concurrency quotas: Allocate explicit concurrency tokens per tenant so a single noisy client cannot monopolize shared server capacity.

The solution. Model arrival burstiness, service variance, and utilization together. Decouple queue wait from execution telemetry, and bound in-flight work with admission control before utilization reaches the nonlinear cliff.

4. Replica Redundancy vs. Independent Bernoulli Trials

Worked Scenario D · Two-replica API with a shared failure domain Illustrative calculation + conditional reliability

The Core Trap: Assuming Servers Fail Like Independent Coin Flips

A classic reliability error is assuming redundant replicas fail independently. If Replica A has an observed unavailability probability of ~1% over an observation window, naive coin-flip math suggests that running two replicas reduces simultaneous failure probability to \(0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0001\) (0.01% downtime, or "Four Nines").

In production cloud topologies, true independence is rare. Replicas share Common-Cause Failure (CCF) domains: top-of-rack switches, power buses, IAM/OIDC identity providers, DNS resolvers, container registries, or shared CI/CD release pipelines.

Corrected Shared-Failure vs. Independent Baseline Math

Consider two active-active replicas serving our gateway API over a defined observation window. We define failures using two distinct event probabilities:

  • Common-cause failure probability (\(q = 0.002\), or 0.2%): The probability of a shared dependency outage taking down both replicas simultaneously over the observation window. (Note: \(q\) is an event probability over this window, not an automatic annual calendar floor; downtime depends on outage duration.)
  • Conditional residual failure probability (\(p = 0.01\), or 1%): The probability that a replica fails independently, given that the common-cause event has not occurred.

Each replica's true marginal unavailability probability (\(p_m\)) is:

$$p_m = q + (1 - q)\,p = 0.002 + (0.998 \times 0.01) = 0.01198 \;(1.198\%)$$

If the two replicas were genuinely independent with this exact same marginal risk, the joint failure probability would be:

$$P_{\text{indep}}(\text{both fail}) = p_m^2 = (0.01198)^2 \approx 0.00014352 \;(0.01435\%)$$

Under the common-cause model, joint failure occurs if the shared dependency fails (\(q\)), or if it survives and both replicas fail independently (\((1-q)p^2\)):

$$P_{\text{ccf}}(\text{both fail}) = q + (1 - q)\,p^2 = 0.002 + (0.998 \times 0.0001) = 0.0020998 \;(0.20998\%)$$ $$\text{Risk Underestimation Factor} = \frac{P_{\text{ccf}}(\text{both fail})}{P_{\text{indep}}(\text{both fail})} = \frac{0.0020998}{0.00014352} \approx \mathbf{14.63\times}$$
Model Configuration Joint Failure Probability Relative Risk Ratio
Independent Replicas (Matched Marginal Risk \(p_m = 1.198\%\)) 0.01435% (\(1.44 \times 10^{-4}\)) \(1.0\times\) (baseline)
Replicas with Shared Failure Mode (\(q = 0.2\%\)) 0.20998% (\(2.10 \times 10^{-3}\)) 14.63× higher risk

(Note on baseline selection: Comparing \(P_{\text{ccf}}\) to \(p^2 = 0.01\%\) yields an apparent 21× ratio, but that comparison artificially drops the shared failure probability from the single-node baseline. Matched marginal risk demonstrates that the true underestimation factor relative to independent nodes is 14.63×.)

Engineering Controls

  • Static stability: Decouple data plane runtime execution from control plane availability. Replicas should cache authorization keys, routing maps, and configuration locally so they continue serving traffic during a central IAM, DNS, or orchestrator outage.
  • Correlated chaos testing: Run fault-injection experiments that sever shared dependencies (or simulate corrupted config rollouts) rather than testing only single-node terminations.

The solution. Define explicit observation windows and model common-cause failures using fault trees or reliability block diagrams. Adding extra replicas yields rapidly diminishing returns unless shared dependency domains are eliminated.

3. Retry Amplification: Bridge to Part 2

Worked Scenario I: Multi-Layer Retry Amplification

Worst-Case Structural Bound AWS Builder's Library grounding

While statistical models describe observations, naive operational controls can change the data-generating process itself during an incident. The most common feedback mechanism is nested retries.

Suppose a request traverses a 5-layer service chain (1: Client → 2: Edge Gateway → 3: Auth Service → 4: Query Router → 5: Storage Worker) calling the Database as the non-retrying terminal dependency. If each of the 5 caller layers independently executes up to \(r = 3\) attempts (original call + 2 retries):

$$N_{\text{attempts}} = r^{L} = 3^{5} = 243 \text{ terminal calls per top-level request}$$

Boundary conditions: The \(3^5 = 243\) multiplier represents a worst-case structural bound under the assumptions that every layer retries the entire downstream call tree, that every attempt fails in a retry-triggering manner, and that all calls reach the terminal dependency. Actual amplification depends on the call topology, retry conditions, cancellation propagation, and deadline expiration. However, as documented in AWS Builder's Library guidance, uncoordinated retries routinely drive systems into metastable overload, where the system remains stalled long after the original trigger clears.

Engineering Controls

  • Single-layer retry ownership: Assign retry authority to exactly one layer (typically the edge gateway or end client); intermediate microservices must fail fast and propagate status codes without retrying.
  • Jitter & retry budgets: Enforce exponential backoff with full jitter to de-correlate retry waves. Limit retry volume using token-bucket retry budgets (e.g., maximum 10% additional traffic over primary requests).

The solution (developed fully in Part 2). Treat retries as dynamic load multipliers. Eliminate nested retry loops, enforce client-side retry budgets, and propagate deadline cancellations down the call stack.

4. Taxonomies of Uncertainty and Fault Modalities

Three Taxonomies of Uncertainty

Treating all uncertainty as generic "noise" leads to flawed architecture. Engineers must distinguish what collecting more data can resolve from what it cannot:

Uncertainty Class Underlying Mechanism Can More Data Fix It? Architectural Mitigation
Aleatory Intrinsic physical or workload randomness (e.g., arrival fluctuations). No. Variance is irreducible. Capacity headroom, statistical multiplexing, and admission shedding.
Epistemic Incomplete knowledge or sparse data (e.g., a newly deployed dependency). Yes. Additional telemetry narrows credible intervals. Canary deployments, shadow traffic, and progressive rollouts.
Model Risk Flawed mathematical assumptions (e.g., assuming independent replica failures). Only if used to falsify the model's structural assumptions. Out-of-sample stress testing, chaos auditing, and static fallback paths.

The Four Modalities of Distributed Faults

Crucial distinction: Crash, omission, timing, and Byzantine behaviors describe fault semantics—how systems fail—not probability distributions. Never assign a textbook curve to a fault class without validating measurable variables:

  • Crash / Fail-Stop: Process halts abruptly. Control: Automated restarts combined with lease fencing tokens at storage layers so partitioned nodes cannot corrupt state.
  • Omission: Silent packet or message drops. Control: Transactional outbox patterns, idempotent mutations, and explicit sequence gap detection.
  • Timing (Performance): Correct payload delivered after caller deadline. Control: Deadline budget propagation (aborting expired downstream work) and status reconciliation.
  • Byzantine: Arbitrary, corrupted, or conflicting behavior. Control: End-to-end payload checksums (CRC32C/SHA-256), cryptographic verification, and formally verified protocols (e.g., TLA+ specifications).

5. Load Testing, Bimodal Mixtures & Fan-Out Amplification

Standard load benchmarks routinely feed systems a sanitized profile: constant arrivals, uniform payloads, and independent requests. Production introduces arrival bursts, skewed payloads, and multitenancy.

Worked Scenario G: Bimodal Mixture Math & Fan-Out Tail Amplification

Mixture moments + fan-out Google "The Tail at Scale" grounding

1. Why the mean describes neither execution path. Suppose a different tenant workload exhibits 80% cache hits (normal with \(\mu_1 = 15\), \(\sigma_1^2 = 16\)) and 20% cache misses (shifted half-normal with \(\mu_2 \approx 94.57\), \(\sigma_2^2 \approx 52.33\)). The exact mixture moments are:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.80(15) + 0.20(94.57) \approx 30.9\ \text{ms}$$ $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = \underbrace{0.80(16) + 0.20(52.33)}_{\text{within-path}} + \underbrace{0.80 \cdot 0.20\,(15 - 94.57)^2}_{\text{between-path}} \approx 1036\ \text{ms}^2 \quad (\sigma \approx 32.2\ \text{ms})$$

The mixture mean of 30.9 ms sits in an empty valley where virtually no queries execute. Between-path variance accounts for over 90% of total variance.

2. Distributed fan-out amplification. When a root query fans out to \(N = 100\) independent downstream shards, each having a \(p = 1\%\) probability of exceeding a deadline:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\text{all 100 children meet deadline}) &= (1-p)^{N} = 0.99^{100} \approx 0.366 \\[6pt] P(\text{parent delayed by} \geq 1 \text{ slow child}) &= 1 - 0.99^{100} \approx 0.634 \;(63.4\%) \end{aligned}$$

As documented in Google’s The Tail at Scale, tail amplification turns rare child stalls into common parent delays.

Engineering Controls on Fan-Out

  • Bounded Hedged Requests: Dispatching a duplicate secondary read after the p95 mark can significantly reduce tail latency. However, hedged requests create extra work. Recommend them only for eligible idempotent operations, where spare capacity exists, with delayed execution, cancellation of losing attempts, and a strictly bounded hedge budget (e.g., maximum 2% extra calls).
  • Partial Results: Return partial data sets only where the application’s business correctness contract explicitly permits partial aggregation.

Reusable Checklist · From Telemetry to an Engineering Decision
  1. Define the Decision & Random Variable: State the exact parameter you are configuring and the measured boundary (e.g., gateway receipt until last byte delivered).
  2. Collect Stratified Telemetry: Capture request class, tenant tier, payload size, cache state, queue wait, service time, retries, and late completions.
  3. Select Candidate Models from the Mechanism: Use empirical quantiles for timeouts, mixture models for distinct paths, queueing models for waiting time, or EVT for rare tail exceedances.
  4. Attempt to Falsify Assumptions: Check quantile errors on held-out windows, temporal autocorrelation, sample size, and whether past failures were censored by tight timeouts.
  5. Implement Architectural Controls: Translate validated models into bounded queues, deadline policies, concurrency limits, retry budgets, or decoupled fallback paths.
  6. Monitor Continuously: Track predicted vs. observed distributions and recalibrate whenever deployments, topologies, or dependencies change.
