The Illusion of Determinism

Breaking the PowerPoint Architecture Script and Translating Math into FinOps

October 17, 2026 · Probability, Queues & Distributed Systems

“If you measure latency using an average, you are measuring the average temperature of the hospital to see if an individual patient is dead.”

— Gil Tene, How NOT to Measure Latency

Every distributed system is, underneath the YAML, RPC stubs, dashboards, and architecture diagrams, a machine operating under uncertainty.

Requests arrive unevenly. Payloads vary by orders of magnitude. Queues accumulate silently. Downstream dependencies stall. Hardware fails unevenly. Retries synchronize into destructive pulses. Tenants exhibit radically different traffic profiles. Components assumed to be independent turn out to share a rack, power domain, top-of-rack switch, hypervisor, control plane, DNS resolver, identity service, or deployment pipeline.

The difficult problem in distributed systems is not merely failure; it is the shape of failure.

Two systems can exhibit identical average latency, identical average CPU utilization, and identical nominal availability while possessing radically different probabilities of catastrophic collapse. One system exhibits thin, predictable tails. The other behaves nominally for weeks, then produces an exceedance event large enough to deplete an entire quarterly error budget in six minutes. The averages are identical; the engineering problems are not.

Good distributed-system design makes uncertainty explicit, measures the assumptions behind it, and creates architectural failure boundaries that remain safe when those assumptions fail.

The Intellectual Spine & The First Rule

The Central Thesis

Do not ask only: "What is the average?"

Ask: "What stochastic process produced this observation? What assumptions does my model make? What empirical evidence supports those assumptions? And what architectural decision changes if those assumptions are wrong?"

The Engineering Workflow
Observed Phenomenon → Data-Generating Process → Random Variable → Assumptions → Candidate Model → Validation → Decision

This is the exact opposite of statistical cargo-culting:

The Anti-Pattern
Incident ⇏ Textbook Distribution ⇏ Prescribed Architecture

A candidate distribution can fit historical telemetry within tight error bounds during stable operating regimes, only to collapse under failure conditions because deployment topologies, retry loops, or network routing altered the underlying data-generating mechanism.

An architect evaluates any model against five operational criteria:

  1. Mechanistically Plausible: It reflects the physical execution path of the runtime.
  2. Empirically Defensible: Production telemetry does not contradict its core quantiles (p95, p99, p99.9).
  3. Decision-Relevant: It estimates the quantity that directly drives an architectural choice.
  4. Robust to Model Error: The system degrades gracefully when reality deviates from the fitted curve.
  5. Continuously Monitorable: Telemetry agents can detect when the workload transitions across statistical regimes.

The First Rule: A Distribution Is a Model, Not a Fact

A production metrics collector records raw timestamps, counters, and durations. It does not record labels stating THIS REQUEST = LOG-NORMAL or THIS FAILURE = POISSON.

Those labels are hypotheses imposed by engineers. Production systems are mixtures of deterministic state machines, stochastic traffic variation, temporal dependencies, common-cause failure couplings, non-linear feedback loops, workload regime changes, and hard physical limits.

The operational question is never: "Which distribution does this metric follow?" It is: "Which model is sufficiently accurate for the decision I need to make?"

Matching the Model to the Decision
  • If the objective is an execution timeout, an empirical percentile is often superior to a fitted log-normal distribution.
  • If the objective is estimating rare exceedances beyond observed history, Extreme Value Theory (EVT) may become appropriate.
  • If the objective is preventing queue collapse, a distribution of service times is insufficient without modeling arrivals, concurrency, utilization, and feedback.
  • If the objective is sizing replica redundancy, assuming independent Bernoulli trials is mathematically reckless because common-cause infrastructure failures dominate.

Three Taxonomies of Uncertainty

Distributed systems operate across three distinct classes of uncertainty. Treating them all as a single "randomness" problem leads to bad architecture.

Aleatory Uncertainty

Intrinsic Randomness. Irreducible variance inherent to the physical environment (e.g., microsecond-level packet jitter across wide-area networks).

Mitigation: Overprovisioning, statistical multiplexing, buffering, admission control.

Epistemic Uncertainty

Incomplete Knowledge. Variance stemming from a lack of system data (e.g., the unknown failure distribution of a new third-party payment gateway under peak traffic).

Mitigation: Active instrumentation, canary deployments, empirical estimation, Bayesian updating.

Model Risk

Structural Mischaracterization. The danger that the chosen mathematical formulation fundamentally misrepresents the operational process (e.g., treating correlated server failures as independent).

Mitigation: Stress testing, chaos engineering, scenario analysis, decoupled fallback architectures.

The Four Modalities of Distributed Faults

Uncertainty manifests structurally in four distinct ways. Recognizing which fault domain your architecture is currently experiencing dictates whether you need a circuit breaker, a retry loop, or formal state verification.

Crash Failures (Fail-Stop)

The component completely halts execution and stays offline. In a fail-stop model, the failure is immediately detectable by peers.

Model via: Bernoulli trials, Weibull degradation.

Omission Failures

The component fails to receive requests or send responses, often due to buffer overflows, dropped packets, or silent network partitions.

Model via: Negative Binomial counts, Poisson arrivals.

Timing Failures (Performance)

The component returns the correct payload, but outside the required temporal window (e.g., GC pauses, cold-start latency, queue saturation).

Model via: Erlang C queues, Extreme Value Theory.

Byzantine Failures

The component acts arbitrarily or maliciously, sending conflicting states to different peers, corrupting the consensus mechanism.

Model via: Quorum Math, TLA+ State Checking.

Preface — In Defense of "It Depends"

A stakeholder asks: "Why is the system slow?"

The accurate engineering answer is often: "The p99 tail has drifted outward because traffic shifted toward an un-indexed cache-miss path, increasing service-time variance. Under our current 100-way synchronous fan-out, that variance is amplified to the point that 63% of parent requests experience tail stalls."

The stakeholder wanted: "Server 4 is slow."

The phrase "it depends" becomes problematic only when treated as an excuse not to quantify the underlying dynamics. Mature systems engineering transforms "it depends" into actionable boundaries:

"It depends on X. Here is the observed range. Here is the probability of exceedance. Here is the operational saturation cliff. And here is the architectural circuit breaker that keeps the system stable when that cliff is reached."

The Illusion of Load Testing

Standard staging benchmarks routinely feed systems a synthetic profile:
Constant Arrival Rate + Homogeneous Payloads + Stable Latency + Independent Calls.

Production reality presents a radically different topology:
Arrival Bursts + Skewed Payloads + Correlated Retries + GC Pauses + Multi-Tenant Contention.

A deterministic benchmark of 1,000 requests per second proves only that the system functions under that specific deterministic trace. It provides zero guarantees of survivability against a burst of 1,000 requests landing in a 50-millisecond window, a coordinated client retry storm, or a whale tenant consuming 60% of shared database IOPS.

Five Nines on a Five-Cent Budget

A target availability of "five nines" (A = 0.99999) permits an annual downtime budget of exactly 5.26 minutes. But downtime math is just trivia if it ignores the economic physics: the cost of each additional "9" scales exponentially, not linearly.

You cannot buy 99.999% by simply provisioning 10% more hardware. You buy it by fundamentally changing the failure domain architecture and removing humans from the critical path.

Operating a cluster at 90% steady-state utilization to minimize infrastructure spend while promising five nines of availability introduces severe fragility. High utilization leaves minimal buffer capacity to absorb stochastic traffic spikes. As queue saturation approaches, waiting time diverges asymptotically. (This is Kingman's formula, which we will explore in Part 3).

Understanding the shape of your failure is the only way to translate infrastructure dollars into actual risk reduction. It's the difference between buying insurance based on actuarial science, and buying insurance based on a magic 8-ball.
