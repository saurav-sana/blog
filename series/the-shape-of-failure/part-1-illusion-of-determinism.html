The Shape of Failure · Part 1 of 8

The Illusion of Determinism

Model uncertainty before turning reliability targets into architecture and FinOps decisions

October 17, 2026 · Probability, Queues & Distributed Systems

“If you measure latency using an average, you are measuring the average temperature of the hospital to see if an individual patient is dead.”

— Gil Tene, How NOT to Measure Latency

Every distributed system is, underneath the YAML, RPC stubs, dashboards, and architecture diagrams, a machine operating under uncertainty.

Requests arrive unevenly. Payloads vary by orders of magnitude. Queues accumulate silently. Downstream dependencies stall. Hardware fails unevenly. Retries synchronize into destructive pulses. Tenants exhibit radically different traffic profiles. Components assumed to be independent turn out to share a rack, power domain, top-of-rack switch, hypervisor, control plane, DNS resolver, identity service, or deployment pipeline.

The difficult problem in distributed systems is not merely failure; it is the shape of failure.

Two systems can exhibit identical average latency, identical average CPU utilization, and identical nominal availability while possessing radically different probabilities of catastrophic collapse. One system exhibits thin, predictable tails. The other behaves nominally for weeks, then produces an exceedance event large enough to deplete an entire quarterly error budget in six minutes. The averages are identical; the engineering problems are not.

Good distributed-system design makes uncertainty explicit, measures the assumptions behind it, and creates architectural failure boundaries that remain safe when those assumptions fail.

The Intellectual Spine & The First Rule

The Central Thesis

Do not ask only: "What is the average?"

Ask: "What stochastic process produced this observation? What assumptions does my model make? What empirical evidence supports those assumptions? And what architectural decision changes if those assumptions are wrong?"

The Engineering Workflow
Observed Phenomenon → Data-Generating Process → Random Variable → Assumptions → Candidate Model → Validation → Decision

This is the exact opposite of statistical cargo-culting:

The Anti-Pattern
Incident ⇏ Textbook Distribution ⇏ Prescribed Architecture

A candidate distribution can fit historical telemetry within tight error bounds during stable operating regimes, only to collapse under failure conditions because deployment topologies, retry loops, or network routing altered the underlying data-generating mechanism.

An architect evaluates any model against five operational criteria:

  1. Mechanistically Plausible: It reflects the physical execution path of the runtime.
  2. Empirically Defensible: Production telemetry does not contradict its core quantiles (p95, p99, p99.9).
  3. Decision-Relevant: It estimates the quantity that directly drives an architectural choice.
  4. Robust to Model Error: The system degrades gracefully when reality deviates from the fitted curve.
  5. Continuously Monitorable: Telemetry agents can detect when the workload transitions across statistical regimes.

The First Rule: A Distribution Is a Model, Not a Fact

A production metrics collector records raw timestamps, counters, and durations. It does not record labels stating THIS REQUEST = LOG-NORMAL or THIS FAILURE = POISSON.

Those labels are hypotheses imposed by engineers. Production systems are mixtures of deterministic state machines, stochastic traffic variation, temporal dependencies, common-cause failure couplings, non-linear feedback loops, workload regime changes, and hard physical limits.

The operational question is never: "Which distribution does this metric follow?" It is: "Which model is sufficiently accurate for the decision I need to make?"

This section is essentially a master guide for architectural decision-making: it warns against applying the wrong mathematical model to a specific engineering problem.

1. Execution Timeouts vs. Log-Normal Distributions

When setting a timeout (e.g., how long a lightweight DuckDB query gateway waits before dropping a request), engineers often try to fit their latency data to a continuous probability curve, like a Log-Normal or Weibull distribution.

The problem is that continuous distributions assume a smooth mathematical decay. Real-world distributed systems are not smooth. They suffer from abrupt physical events: garbage collection (GC) pauses, network retries, or hypervisor context switches. A fitted Log-Normal curve can smooth over these realities and produce a misleading timeout estimate if the data mixes distinct execution paths or regime changes. A continuous model is not inherently wrong; it must be tested against the mechanism and the tail quantiles that matter.

The Solution Start with empirical percentiles (for example, HDR Histogram data) and compare them with candidate models. Empirical p99.9 is evidence about the observed workload, not a guarantee about unobserved extremes: its reliability depends on sample size, independence, histogram precision, censoring, and whether the workload regime remains comparable. Set timeouts within caller deadlines, propagate cancellation, and budget for retries so abandoned work does not keep consuming capacity.
2. Rare Exceedances vs. Extreme Value Theory (EVT)

A convenient distribution fitted to the center of observed data can underestimate rare extremes when its tail assumptions do not match the generating process. Normal and Poisson models are not universally unsuitable; the risk comes from applying an unvalidated tail model to a different question or regime.

The Solution When the decision concerns rare exceedances beyond the bulk of observed data, Extreme Value Theory (EVT) may be useful—for example, block-maxima or peaks-over-threshold methods. EVT does not reveal a guaranteed maximum or make extrapolation automatically accurate. Check threshold choice, sample size, dependence, regime stability, uncertainty intervals, and out-of-sample behavior before using tail estimates to set controls.
3. Queue Collapse vs. Modeling Arrivals

If you are designing a high-throughput system (like an Apache Iceberg reconciliation engine), simply knowing the distribution of how fast a node processes a request (Service Time) is useless on its own.

In stable queueing models, waiting time can rise sharply as utilization approaches 100%, especially when arrival and service-time variability are high. Kingman’s approximation describes a single-server FCFS GI/G/1 queue under its assumptions; it is not a universal formula for every multi-server, admission-controlled, or overloaded system. If you only model service time, you can miss the interaction between arrivals, variability, and concurrency.

The Solution To understand queueing behavior, model the arrival process (Poisson arrivals are a useful baseline, but do not by themselves represent bursty traffic), the service-time distribution, available concurrency, utilization, admission control, and feedback loops such as retries that multiply offered load during a stall.
4. Replica Redundancy vs. Bernoulli Trials

A Bernoulli trial is a classic coin flip: the outcome of one flip has absolutely no effect on the next. Historically, architects sized replica redundancy using this math. They assumed if Server A has a 1% chance of failing, and Server B has a 1% chance of failing, the chance of both failing is 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001.

In modern cloud environments, independence is an assumption to verify, not a default. Replicas may share a top-of-rack switch, power domain, identity service, DNS resolver, deployment pipeline, or control plane. Whether a shared dependency can fail both replicas depends on topology, dependency paths, and the failure mode.

The Solution Model common-cause failures explicitly. For a simple illustration, let q be the probability of a shared outage and p the residual failure probability of each replica, assumed independent conditional on no shared outage. Then P(both fail) = q + (1 − q)p². This is only a simplified model: real systems may have multiple shared dependencies and conditional dependencies. The point is to identify and test the common failure paths, not to assume they make joint failure certain.

Three Taxonomies of Uncertainty

Distributed systems operate across three distinct classes of uncertainty. Treating them all as a single "randomness" problem leads to bad architecture.

Aleatory Uncertainty

Intrinsic Randomness. Irreducible variance inherent to the physical environment (e.g., microsecond-level packet jitter across wide-area networks).

Mitigation: Overprovisioning, statistical multiplexing, buffering, admission control.

Epistemic Uncertainty

Incomplete Knowledge. Variance stemming from a lack of system data (e.g., the unknown failure distribution of a new third-party payment gateway under peak traffic).

Mitigation: Active instrumentation, canary deployments, empirical estimation, Bayesian updating.

Model Risk

Structural Mischaracterization. The danger that the chosen mathematical formulation fundamentally misrepresents the operational process (e.g., treating correlated server failures as independent).

Mitigation: Stress testing, chaos engineering, scenario analysis, decoupled fallback architectures.

The Four Modalities of Distributed Faults

Fault models describe how a component can misbehave; probability distributions describe uncertainty about measured quantities. They are related but not interchangeable. Recognizing the fault semantics helps choose controls such as timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, quorum protocols, or formal state verification; the statistical model must then be selected separately and validated.

Crash Failures (Fail-Stop)

The component completely halts execution and stays offline. In a fail-stop model, the failure is immediately detectable by peers.

Analyze with: explicit crash-state assumptions; estimate crash frequency or time-to-failure separately when data supports it. Bernoulli or Weibull models are candidates, not definitions of a crash.

Omission Failures

The component fails to receive requests or send responses, often due to buffer overflows, dropped packets, or silent network partitions.

Analyze with: message-loss, timeout, and partition observations. Poisson or Negative Binomial models may describe selected counts only if their assumptions fit the data.

Timing Failures (Performance)

The component returns the correct payload, but outside the required temporal window (e.g., GC pauses, cold-start latency, queue saturation).

Analyze with: deadline-miss rates, latency distributions, and a queueing model matched to the service discipline and workload. Erlang C or EVT may be useful for specific questions, not as fault definitions.

Byzantine Failures

The component acts arbitrarily or maliciously, sending conflicting states to different peers, corrupting the consensus mechanism.

Analyze with: an explicit adversarial fault model, protocol/quorum reasoning, and formal verification where appropriate. Byzantine behavior is not adequately represented by assigning it an ordinary latency distribution.

Preface — How to Talk to Business (In Defense of "It Depends")

A VP of Product drops into the incident Slack channel: "Why is checkout slow?"

The accurate engineering answer is usually a beautiful, terrifying truth: "The p99 tail has drifted outward because a thundering herd shifted traffic toward an un-indexed cache-miss path, increasing service-time variance. With our 100-way synchronous fan-out, a parent request completes only when its slowest child completes."

The VP of Product blinks. They just wanted: "Server 4 is on fire. We rebooted it."

The phrase "it depends" is the most hated phrase in business. It sounds like an excuse to evade accountability. But in distributed systems, "it depends" is literally the physics of the environment.

To bridge this gap, mature engineering transforms "it depends" from a defensive shrug into an actionable business boundary. You don't teach them queueing theory; you give them the levers of probability.

The Translation Framework

Instead of stopping at "It depends," translate the math into FinOps and UX decisions:

  • The Variable: "It depends on [Business Lever]—e.g., how many free-tier users we allow to queue."
  • The Range: "Here is the observed range of behavior during peak hours."
  • The Saturation Cliff: "If traffic hits this specific threshold, the system collapses."
  • The Business Choice: "To keep the system stable when that cliff is reached, our circuit breaker will drop premium analytics. Do you approve this fallback?"

The Illusion of Load Testing

Standard staging benchmarks routinely feed systems a synthetic profile:
Constant Arrival Rate + Homogeneous Payloads + Stable Latency + Independent Calls.

Production reality presents a radically different topology:
Arrival Bursts + Skewed Payloads + Correlated Retries + GC Pauses + Multi-Tenant Contention.

A benchmark of 1,000 requests per second provides evidence about the tested trace, not a guarantee of behavior under a burst of 1,000 requests in a 50-millisecond window, a coordinated client retry storm, or a tenant consuming 60% of shared database IOPS. Those scenarios change arrival concentration, contention, or both, and should be tested explicitly.

Five Nines: Availability Budgets and the Cost of Resilience

A target availability of "five nines" (A = 0.99999) corresponds to about 5.26 minutes of unavailability in a 365-day year. This is an availability budget, not a prediction of actual downtime. The engineering and economic question is how much it costs to reduce the probability and duration of outages for this particular service, workload, and failure model.

More hardware alone does not guarantee higher availability. Depending on the dominant failure modes, improvement may require independent failure domains, tested failover, faster detection and recovery, safer deployments, dependency isolation, capacity reserves, or reducing manual steps in the recovery path. No single architecture—active-active, consensus, or otherwise—automatically guarantees a particular availability target.

Use service-specific cost and incident data to replace the index before making a budget decision.

The broader engineering lesson is consistent with Site Reliability Engineering: define a service-level objective, make the associated error budget explicit, and evaluate reliability work against user impact and opportunity cost. The marginal cost of another nine can be high, but whether it is justified is a service-specific business decision—not a universal theorem.

Operating near 90% steady-state utilization can leave limited headroom for bursts and recovery work. In stable queues, waiting time often rises nonlinearly as utilization increases; the exact behavior depends on arrival variability, service-time variability, queue discipline, concurrency, and admission control. Kingman’s approximation is one useful single-server result, not a universal law for every architecture. See Little’s queueing identity for a related conservation relationship; it does not itself specify a waiting-time distribution. Part 3 will examine the queueing assumptions and operational controls in more detail.

Understanding the shape of your failure is the only way to translate infrastructure dollars into actual risk reduction. It's the difference between buying insurance based on actuarial science, and buying insurance based on a magic 8-ball.
