The Shape of Failure · Part 1 of 8

The Illusion of Determinism

Model uncertainty before turning reliability targets into architecture and FinOps decisions

October 17, 2026 · Probability, Queues & Distributed Systems

“If you measure latency using an average, you are measuring the average temperature of the hospital to see if an individual patient is dead.”

— Gil Tene, How NOT to Measure Latency

Every distributed system is, underneath the YAML, RPC stubs, dashboards, and suspiciously clean architecture diagrams, a machine operating under uncertainty.

Requests arrive unevenly. Payloads vary by orders of magnitude. Queues accumulate silently between polling intervals. Downstream dependencies stall. Hardware fails unevenly. Retries synchronize into destructive pulses. Tenants exhibit radically different traffic profiles. Components assumed to be independent on a whiteboard turn out to share a rack, power domain, top-of-rack switch, hypervisor, control plane, DNS resolver, identity service, or deployment pipeline.

The difficult problem in distributed systems is not merely failure; it is the shape of failure.

Two systems can exhibit identical average latency, identical average CPU utilization, and identical nominal availability while possessing radically different probabilities of catastrophic collapse. One system exhibits thin, predictable tails. The other behaves nominally for weeks, then produces an exceedance event large enough to deplete an entire quarterly error budget in six minutes. The averages are identical; the engineering problems are not.

Good distributed-system design makes uncertainty explicit, measures the assumptions behind it, and creates architectural failure boundaries that remain safe when those assumptions fail.

Recurring Architecture Spine & Epistemic Honesty

To keep this guide grounded in real systems rather than textbook trivia, we will trace one recurring production architecture across every section: a Multi-Tenant Analytics Gateway (stateless DuckDB + Apache Arrow query workers reading Apache Iceberg tables over object storage, with Redis metadata caching and multi-AZ routing).

A note on evidence: Throughout this article, we explicitly distinguish between Documented Industry Evidence (published findings from Google SRE, AWS, and systems literature) and Illustrative Engineering Calculations (reproducible mathematical scenarios showing how to compute parameters, validate models, and size controls).

The Intellectual Spine & The First Rule

The Central Thesis

Do not ask only: "What is the average?"

Ask: "What stochastic process produced this observation? What exact random variable am I measuring? What assumptions does my model make? What empirical evidence falsifies those assumptions? And what architectural decision changes if those assumptions are wrong?"

The Engineering Workflow
Observed Phenomenon → Data-Generating Process → Random Variable → Assumptions → Candidate Model → Validation → Decision

This is the exact opposite of statistical cargo-culting:

The Anti-Pattern
Incident ⇏ Textbook Distribution ⇏ Prescribed Architecture

A candidate distribution can fit historical telemetry within tight error bounds during stable operating regimes, only to collapse under failure conditions because deployment topologies, retry loops, or network routing altered the underlying data-generating mechanism.

An architect evaluates any model against five operational criteria:

  1. Mechanistically Plausible: It reflects the physical execution path of the runtime.
  2. Empirically Defensible: Production telemetry does not contradict its core quantiles (p95, p99, p99.9) on out-of-sample windows.
  3. Decision-Relevant: It estimates the quantity that directly drives an architectural choice (timeout, buffer size, worker concurrency, or replica count).
  4. Robust to Model Error: The system degrades gracefully when reality deviates from the fitted curve.
  5. Continuously Monitorable: Telemetry agents can detect when the workload transitions across statistical regimes.

The First Rule: A Distribution Is a Model, Not a Fact

A production metrics collector records raw timestamps, counters, and durations. It does not record labels stating THIS REQUEST = LOG-NORMAL or THIS FAILURE = POISSON.

Those labels are hypotheses imposed by engineers. Production systems are mixtures of deterministic state machines, stochastic traffic variation, temporal dependencies, common-cause failure couplings, non-linear feedback loops, workload regime changes, and hard physical limits.

The operational question is never: "Which distribution does this metric follow?" It is: "Which model is sufficiently accurate for the decision I need to make?"

Four Places Where Textbook Models Collide with Production

Below are the four classic places where unvalidated textbook models collide with production physics—paired with reproducible calculations and concrete engineering controls.

1. Execution Timeouts vs. Log-Normal & Weibull Distributions

Worked Scenario A · Gateway timeout sizing & censoring Illustrative calculation + Google SRE grounding

When setting a timeout (e.g., how long our lightweight DuckDB query gateway waits before dropping a request), engineers often try to fit their latency data to a single continuous probability curve, like a Log-Normal or Weibull distribution.

Neither distribution is inherently inappropriate for latency within a single regime. The trap is that continuous unimodal curves assume a single smooth decay, whereas real-world systems execute across finite mixtures of distinct paths: fast memory cache hits, cold object-store reads, garbage collection (GC) pauses, and hypervisor stalls. Fitting one Log-Normal curve across mixed execution paths smooths over multimodal cliffs and yields a dangerous timeout estimate.

Random variable & setup. Let \(X\) be downstream query latency (ms) measured from request dispatch to last response byte on our multi-tenant analytics gateway. Most queries hit local Arrow/Redis caches, while about 15% require object-store reads and metadata lookups. Suppose 1,000,000 representative requests yield these empirical quantiles versus an unsegmented Log-Normal fit:

Metric Measured HDR Histogram Unsegmented Log-Normal Fit Operational Interpretation
p50 (median)18 ms19 msDominated by hot cache-hit population.
p9595 ms68 msUnimodal fit underestimates transition to cold S3 reads.
p99180 ms112 msMulti-partition object-store reads + metadata lock contention.
p99.9280 ms190 msTail regime; naive fit misses empirical p99.9 by 32%.
$$\text{Exceedances above 280 ms (p99.9)} = 1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.001 = 1{,}000 \text{ requests per million}$$

Why 280 ms is an investigation starting point, not an automatic timeout. If you set a hard downstream timeout at 280 ms, roughly 1,000 requests per million are cut off. That does not mean 1,000 requests would have failed—many are legitimate cold-cache queries that would have succeeded at 295 ms. Worse, if the timeout censors the measurement without cancelling the backend worker, immediate retries create up to 1,000 additional downstream attempts for those 1,000 timed-out requests, while actual concurrency depends on cancellation propagation, retry timing, concurrency limits, and how long the original work continues running.

  • Engineering control 1 (population separation): Segment HDR Histograms by cache_state, tenant_tier, and query_class before evaluating tail quantiles.
  • Engineering control 2 (deadline & cancellation propagation): Propagate remaining caller deadline (grpc-timeout / X-Deadline-Ms) and wire socket close events to duckdb_interrupt() so timed-out queries immediately release CPU and memory.
  • Engineering control 3 (retry accounting): Track timeout-induced retry rates alongside raw timeout counts to detect when a tight timeout is amplifying load.

The solution. Start with stratified empirical percentiles (e.g., HDR Histogram data separated by cache hit/miss and request class) and test candidate curves against out-of-sample tail quantiles. Account for timeout censoring, set timeouts within caller deadlines, propagate cancellation, and budget for retries so abandoned work does not keep consuming capacity.

2. Rare Exceedances vs. Extreme Value Theory (EVT)

Worked Scenario B · Sizing burst headroom with the Generalized Pareto Illustrative calculation + NIST statistical reference

A convenient distribution fitted to the center of observed data (such as a Gaussian or standard Poisson model) can severely underestimate rare extremes when its tail assumptions do not match the data-generating process. Normal and Poisson models are not universally wrong; the danger comes from extrapolating a center-fitted model to answer a high-consequence tail question.

Random variable & setup. Suppose our analytics platform experiences periodic latency spikes during heavy reporting windows, and the team must decide how much worker capacity and deadline headroom to reserve. We apply a Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) analysis above threshold \(u = 500\,\text{ms}\), where 1% (\(0.01\)) of all requests exceed 500 ms. Suppose fitting a Generalized Pareto Distribution to the exceedances \(Y = X - u\) yields shape parameter \(\xi = 0.2\) (heavy-tailed power-law decay) and scale parameter \(\beta = 100\,\text{ms}\). The conditional survival function is:

$$P(Y > y \mid X > u) = \left(1 + \frac{\xi\,y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi}$$

For \(X > 1000\) ms (exceedance \(y = 1000 - 500 = 500\) ms):

$$\begin{aligned} P(X > 1000 \mid X > 500) &= \left(1 + \frac{0.2 \times 500}{100}\right)^{-1/0.2} = 2^{-5} = 0.03125 \;(3.125\%) \\[6pt] P(X > 1000) &= 0.01 \times 0.03125 = 0.0003125 \;(\approx 313 \text{ per million requests}) \end{aligned}$$

Why this matters. The fitted model estimates approximately 313 requests per million exceed 1 second under the stated assumptions, emphasizing that an estimated exceedance probability is a model output to validate against held-out observations and uncertainty intervals, not a guarantee.

  • Falsification & validation: Check threshold sensitivity across \(u \in [400, 650]\) ms, de-cluster temporally correlated spikes (so one 5-second storage hiccup isn't treated as 200 independent samples), and test predictions against held-out stress periods. Refit after major deployments.
  • Engineering control: Use GPD return-level estimates to configure load-shedding thresholds, isolated heavy-query bulkhead lanes, and byte-weighted memory admission semaphores—never as a contractual promise of "maximum possible latency."

The solution. When the decision concerns rare exceedances beyond the bulk of observed data, Extreme Value Theory (EVT)—using Block Maxima or Peaks-Over-Threshold (Generalized Pareto Distribution, GPD)—provides a principled tail model. EVT does not reveal a guaranteed maximum or make extrapolation automatically accurate: verify threshold stability, de-cluster dependent bursts, and check confidence intervals before sizing capacity reserves.

3. Queue Collapse vs. Modeling Arrivals & Utilization

Worked Scenario C · The utilization cliff in a single-server queue Illustrative calculation + queueing physics

If you are designing a high-throughput service (like an Apache Iceberg manifest reconciliation engine or query worker pool), knowing the standalone distribution of how fast a worker processes a request (service time \(S\)) is useless on its own.

In stable queueing models, waiting time rises sharply and nonlinearly as utilization \(\rho\) approaches 100%, amplified by arrival and service-time variability. Kingman’s approximation describes a single-server FCFS GI/G/1 queue under stability assumptions; it is not a direct formula for a multi-server worker pool, admission-controlled queue, or overloaded system. Yet if you only model service time, you miss the explosive interaction between arrival bursts, service variance, and utilization.

Random variable & setup. Consider a single serialized commit queue where mean service time is \(\mathbb{E}[S] = 10\) ms (capacity \(\mu = 100\) requests/s). Assume arrivals are approximately Poisson (squared coefficient of variation of interarrival times \(c_a^2 = 1\)) and service-time variability is \(c_s^2 = 1\). Under Kingman’s GI/G/1 approximation:

$$W_q \approx \frac{c_a^2 + c_s^2}{2} \cdot \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} \cdot \mathbb{E}[S]$$
Utilization \(\rho\) Arrival rate \(\lambda\) Approx. mean queue wait \(W_q\) Total response time \(W = W_q + \mathbb{E}[S]\) Mean in-flight work \(L = \lambda W\)
50%50 req/s10 ms20 ms1.0 request
80%80 req/s40 ms50 ms4.0 requests
90%90 req/s90 ms100 ms9.0 requests
95%95 req/s190 ms (\(19 \times \mathbb{E}[S]\)!)200 ms19.0 requests

Scope & nuance. At 95% utilization, a tiny 10 ms task spends 190 ms waiting in line—and if arrivals are burstier than Poisson (e.g., synchronized client cron jobs with \(c_a^2 = 4\)), that wait multiplies another 2.5×! Note that 90% utilization is not a universal failure threshold across all systems, and multi-worker gateways require multi-server models (GI/G/c) or discrete-event simulation validated against production traces.

  • Engineering control 1 (bounded queues & admission control): Never allow unbounded FIFO queues. Reject or defer work when expected queue wait exceeds the caller's deadline.
  • Engineering control 2 (separate telemetry & Little's Law reconciliation): Instrument queue_wait_ms separately from service_exec_ms, and use Little’s Law (\(L = \lambda W\)) to reconcile throughput, in-flight concurrency, and latency over consistent windows.
  • Engineering control 3 (per-tenant concurrency quotas): Reserve concurrency slots per tenant and query class so one noisy tenant cannot push shared utilization past the nonlinear cliff.

The solution. Model the arrival process (homogeneous Poisson is a baseline, not a law for bursty traffic), the service-time distribution, utilization \(\rho\), concurrency limits via Little’s Law (\(L = \lambda W\)), and feedback loops such as retries that multiply offered load during a stall.

4. Replica Redundancy vs. Independent Bernoulli Trials

Worked Scenario D · Two-replica API with a shared failure domain Illustrative calculation + conditional reliability

A Bernoulli trial is a classic coin flip: the outcome of one flip has zero causal effect on the next. Historically, architects sized replica redundancy using this math. They assumed if Server A has a 1% probability of being unavailable during an observation interval, and Server B has a 1% probability, the probability of both failing is \(0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0001\).

In modern cloud environments, independence is a strong hypothesis to verify, not a default. Replicas frequently share a top-of-rack switch, power domain, OIDC identity service, DNS resolver, schema migration lock, or CI/CD deployment pipeline.

Random variable & setup. Consider two active-active replicas serving our gateway API over a defined observation window. Let \(q = 0.002\) (0.2%) be the probability of a common-cause outage during that interval. We define \(p = 0.01\) (1%) strictly as the conditional residual unavailability probability, given that the common-cause event has not occurred. Each replica's marginal unavailability is therefore \(q + (1-q)p = 0.01198\) (1.198%).

$$\begin{aligned} P_{\text{indep}}(\text{both fail}) &= p^2 = 0.01^2 = 0.0001 \;(0.01\%) \\[6pt] P(\text{one replica down}) &= q + (1-q)\,p = 0.002 + 0.998 \times 0.01 = 0.01198 \;(1.198\%) \\[6pt] P_{\text{ccf}}(\text{both fail}) &= q + (1-q)\,p^2 = 0.002 + 0.998 \times 0.0001 = 0.0020998 \;(0.20998\%) \end{aligned}$$ $$\text{Risk underestimation factor} = \frac{0.0020998}{0.0001} \approx 21\times$$

Architectural takeaway. Because \(q = 0.002\) acts as an availability floor, adding a 3rd, 4th, or 10th replica barely moves the needle (\(P \to q = 0.002\)). Real systems may require fault trees or reliability block diagrams with multiple shared failure-domain states.

  • Engineering control 1 (failure-domain mapping & static stability): Map replicas against physical and logical dependencies (zones, racks, DNS, IAM, control planes). Cache identity keys and routing state locally (static stability) so the data plane survives a control-plane outage.
  • Engineering control 2 (correlated chaos testing): Run fault-injection experiments that sever a shared dependency (or simulate a bad config rollout), verifying that failover paths do not depend on the exact same broken component as the primary path.

The solution. Define the exact observation interval and model common-cause failures explicitly. Identify and fault-inject shared dependency paths rather than assuming extra replicas automatically multiply your nines.

5. Preview of Part 2: Retry Amplification & Feedback Loops

Worked Scenario I · Multi-layer retry amplification AWS Builder's Library grounding

Part 1 focuses on how statistical models describe observations. Part 2 addresses an even more dangerous phenomenon: how naive operational controls change the data-generating process itself during a stall.

Suppose a request traverses a 5-layer service chain (1: Client → 2: Edge Gateway → 3: Auth Service → 4: Query Router → 5: Storage Worker) calling the Database as the non-retrying terminal dependency. Each of the 5 caller layers independently makes up to 3 attempts (the original call + 2 retries) when a downstream dependency slows down:

$$N_{\text{attempts}} = r^{L} = 3^{5} = 243 \text{ terminal database calls per top-level request}$$

This is a worst-case structural bound when every attempt fails in a retry-triggering manner, not an expected average amplification factor in every incident. However, as AWS’s published guidance on timeouts, retries, and backoff warns, independent multi-layer retries multiply load on an already failing dependency and lock systems into metastable overload.

Engineering controls (developed fully in Part 2). Assign retry ownership to a single designated layer, enforce end-to-end deadlines, use exponential backoff with full jitter, retry only transient errors on idempotent operations (with idempotency keys), cap retry volume with client-side retry budgets, and monitor original attempts separately from retry attempts.

Three Taxonomies of Uncertainty

Distributed systems operate across three distinct classes of uncertainty. Treating them all as a single "randomness" problem leads to bad architecture. Specifically, engineers must know what collecting more telemetry can solve—and what it cannot.

Aleatory uncertainty — intrinsic randomness

Irreducible variance inherent to the workload even after years of observation.

Case Study E1 (traffic variation). An established API receives 10,000 req/min on average (about 166.7 req/s), but actual per-second arrivals fluctuate wildly due to uncoordinated user bursts. Collecting five more years of logs will not make every second contain exactly 167 requests.

Can more data fix it? No.
Controls: Capacity headroom, bounded queues, statistical multiplexing, autoscaling with lead time, and admission control based on empirical burst distributions.

Epistemic uncertainty — incomplete knowledge

Uncertainty stemming from sparse data on an unfamiliar dependency or unexercised regime.

Case Study E2 (new dependency). A newly integrated payment gateway exhibits 2 failures in 100 calls (2% observed rate). With only 100 trials, the true failure rate is not known to be 2%—its confidence/credible interval is still wide.

Can more data fix it? Yes.
Controls: Canary traffic, shadow testing, Bayesian credible-interval updating, strict dependency timeouts, and progressive rollout.

Model risk — structural mischaracterization

The chosen mathematical equation fundamentally misrepresents the operational mechanism.

Case Study E3 (broken independence). Multiplying failure probabilities (\(p^2\)) for two replicas that share a single regional identity provider (Scenario D), or fitting a unimodal curve to a bimodal cache/storage path.

Can more data fix it? Only if used to falsify the model.
Controls: Failure-mode & dependency-graph analysis, out-of-sample quantile checks, chaos experiments, and decoupled fallbacks.

The Four Modalities of Distributed Faults

Crucial distinction: Crash, omission, timing, and Byzantine behaviors are fault semantics—not probability distributions. Never assign a textbook distribution to a fault class automatically. First identify the fault semantic, define the measurable random variable, and then validate a separate statistical or formal protocol model.

1. Crash / fail-stop failures

Scenario: A query worker process terminates abruptly (e.g., OOM-kill or segfault). Nuance: True fail-stop is a stronger assumption than merely crashing—it assumes peers can reliably detect that the process halted rather than stalled.

What to measure/model: Crash rate per window, time-to-failure (Weibull/exponential only if supported by telemetry), and recovery duration.

Primary controls: Health detection with lease fencing tokens, automated restart/rescheduling, and idempotent work recovery.

2. Omission failures

Scenario: Requests or responses disappear silently during a network glitch, NIC buffer drop, or asymmetric partition without crashing the node.

What to measure/model: Loss counts, timeout rates, retry outcomes, and partition durations (Poisson or burst-loss models apply only if assumptions fit).

Primary controls: Bounded retries with deadlines, durable messaging/outbox patterns, sequence numbers, and state reconciliation.

3. Timing failures (performance)

Scenario: A worker responds with a valid payload, but after the caller's deadline has expired due to GC pauses, cold starts, or queue saturation.

What to measure/model: End-to-end latency distribution, deadline-miss rate, and queue wait time \(W_q\) separate from execution time.

Primary controls: Admission control, capacity reserves, deadline propagation, cooperative cancellation, and ambiguity resolution (did the timed-out op commit?).

4. Byzantine failures

Scenario: A buggy, bit-flipped, or compromised participant sends conflicting state or corrupted payloads to different peers.

What to measure/model: Protocol safety invariants, conflicting message counts, and adversarial threat bounds—not a latency histogram.

Primary controls: Cryptographic authentication/checksums, quorum/BFT protocol reasoning, strict state validation, and formal verification (TLA+).

Preface — How to Talk to Business (In Defense of "It Depends")

A VP of Product drops into the incident Slack channel: "Why is checkout slow?"

The accurate engineering answer is usually a beautiful, terrifying truth: "The p99 tail has drifted outward because a thundering herd shifted traffic toward an un-indexed cache-miss path, increasing service-time variance. With our 100-way synchronous fan-out, a parent request completes only when its slowest child completes."

The VP of Product blinks. They just wanted: "Server 4 is on fire. We rebooted it."

The phrase "it depends" is the most hated phrase in business. It sounds like an excuse to evade accountability. But in distributed systems, "it depends" is literally the physics of the environment.

To bridge this gap, mature engineering transforms vague statements like "the system collapses" into measurable operational boundaries: queue depth, admission rejection rate, deadline-miss rate, or backlog drain time.

The Translation Framework

Instead of stopping at "It depends," translate the math into measurable FinOps and UX decisions:

  • The Variable: "It depends on [Business Lever]—e.g., the ratio of uncached ad-hoc queries we allow free-tier tenants to run concurrently."
  • The Measurable Range: "At our normal 80% cache-hit ratio, p99 latency stays below 180 ms and queue wait is under 20 ms."
  • The Saturation Boundary: "If burst traffic pushes worker utilization past 90% (queue depth > 64), deadline-miss rates exceed 15% and backlog drain time exceeds 4 minutes."
  • The Business Choice: "To protect checkout SLAs when queue depth hits 64, our admission controller will shed free-tier analytics with HTTP 429. Do you approve this policy?"

The Illusion of Load Testing, Mixtures & Correlated Bursts

Standard staging benchmarks routinely feed systems a clean synthetic profile: constant arrival rate + homogeneous payloads + stable latency + independent calls.

Production reality often presents a harsher regime: arrival bursts + skewed payloads + correlated retries + GC pauses + multi-tenant contention.

Not every production workload is bursty, and not every benchmark is useless—rather, a benchmark establishes evidence only for the workload and conditions tested. Furthermore, closed-loop load generators (where virtual users wait for a response before sending the next request) automatically throttle themselves when the system slows down, hiding overload unless you test with open-loop arrival schedules.

Worked Scenario G: Bimodal Mixture Math & Fan-Out Tail Amplification

Mixture moments + fan-out Google "The Tail at Scale" grounding

1. Why the mean describes neither execution path. Suppose 80% of gateway requests are cache hits (normal with \(\mu_1 = 15\), \(\sigma_1^2 = 16\)) and 20% are cache misses (an 85 ms shift plus a half-normal tail, giving \(\mu_2 \approx 94.57\), \(\sigma_2^2 \approx 52.33\)). The exact mixture moments are:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.80(15) + 0.20(94.57) \approx 30.9\ \text{ms}$$ $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = \underbrace{0.80(16) + 0.20(52.33)}_{\text{within-path}} + \underbrace{0.80 \cdot 0.20\,(15 - 94.57)^2}_{\text{between-path}} \approx 1036\ \text{ms}^2 \quad (\sigma \approx 32.2\ \text{ms})$$

The quantiles follow the mixture: \(p_{50} \approx 16\) ms and \(p_{95} \approx 99\) ms. Notice that the mean of 30.9 ms sits in an empty valley where essentially no requests actually execute, and that almost all of the variance comes from the gap between the two paths, not from noise within either one.

2. Distributed fan-out amplification. Now suppose a parent query fans out to \(N = 100\) independent downstream shard calls, and each child call has just a \(p = 1\%\) probability of exceeding a target deadline:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\text{all 100 children finish within deadline}) &= (1-p)^{N} = 0.99^{100} \approx 0.366 \\[6pt] P(\text{parent delayed by} \geq 1 \text{ slow child}) &= 1 - 0.99^{100} \approx 0.634 \end{aligned}$$

This is the exact mathematical mechanism documented in Google’s The Tail at Scale, which reported a real production service where the 99th-percentile latency was 10 ms for a single leaf request, but jumped to 140 ms when waiting for all fanned-out requests to finish. Engineering controls: Bound fan-out width and concurrency, test with open-loop burst generators, and use partial results or hedged requests (dispatching a secondary read after the p95 mark) where idempotency permits.

Five Nines: Availability Budgets and the Economics of Resilience

A target availability of "five nines" (\(A = 0.99999\)) corresponds to about 5.256 minutes of unavailability in a 365-day year. Using calendar arithmetic, the annual unavailability budget \(D\) is:

$$\begin{aligned} D &= (1 - A) \times 365 \times 24 \times 60 \\ &= (1 - 0.99999) \times 525{,}600 \approx 5.256 \text{ minutes per year} \end{aligned}$$

Reusable Checklist · From Telemetry to an Engineering Decision
  1. Define the Decision & Random Variable: State the exact control you are configuring and the measured boundary (e.g., gateway receipt of request until last response byte delivered).
  2. Collect Representative, Stratified Telemetry: Capture request class, tenant, payload size, cache state, deployment version, queue wait, service time, retries, cancellations, and outcome.
  3. Select Candidate Models from the Mechanism: Use empirical quantiles for timeouts, mixture models for distinct execution paths, queueing models/simulation for waiting time, or EVT for supported tail-exceedance questions.
  4. Validate & Attempt to Falsify Assumptions: Check quantile errors (p95/p99/p99.9), temporal autocorrelation, workload shifts, sample size, and whether failures were censored by existing timeouts.
  5. Make the Architectural Decision: Translate the validated model into a bounded queue, deadline policy, concurrency limit, reserved capacity buffer, retry budget, or decoupled fallback.
  6. Monitor & Revalidate Continuously: Compare predicted outcomes with subsequent production observations and refit whenever deployments, traffic profiles, topologies, or dependencies change.

Understanding the shape of your failure is the only way to translate infrastructure dollars into actual risk reduction. It's the difference between buying insurance based on actuarial science, and buying insurance based on a magic 8-ball.
