The Physics of Time, Traffic, and Queues

October 24, 2026 · Part 3 of 8

“Utilization is not a target. It is a limit.”

— Baron Schwartz, Systems Performance Author

Queueing theory is the math that explains why 95% CPU utilization means your database is technically alive, but practically experiencing the heat death of the universe. As saturation approaches 100%, queue delay doesn't scale linearly—it diverges asymptotically. Your latency doesn't just double; it tends toward infinity.

To understand why, we have to model the three distinct physical mechanics of any queue: how things arrive, how long they take to serve, and what happens when you chain them together.

1. The Poisson Distribution (Arrivals)

The Intuition: The Bus Stop

Poisson models uncoordinated arrivals. Think of a bus stop in a major city. Buses don't arrive on a perfect 10-minute schedule. You wait, nobody comes for 20 minutes, and then three buses arrive at the exact same time.

In distributed systems, this is the traffic hitting your load balancer. It assumes arrivals are independent. One user clicking "buy" doesn't mathematically dictate when the next user clicks. But when traffic spikes, they cluster. Your system must be sized to handle the variance of these bursts, not just the average rate.

The Math

The probability of observing exactly k arrivals in a fixed time window, given an average arrival rate of λ:

P(X = k) = (λk · e-λ) / k!
Let's decode this:
  • λ (Lambda): The average arrival rate (e.g., 50 requests per second).
  • k: The actual number of requests you are calculating the probability for.
  • e: Euler's number (approx 2.718).
  • k!: Factorial (e.g., 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24).

Worked Example: If your average rate is 10 requests/sec (λ = 10), what is the chance of getting exactly 15 requests in one second? The formula gives roughly 3.4%. What about 30 requests in one second? The probability drops to almost zero. But if you get 100 servers all bursting at once, those "almost zero" events become your daily reality.

The SRE Trap

The Poisson distribution assumes arrivals are completely uncoordinated. But the moment you add a "retry button" to your frontend, or a cron job that runs at exactly 12:00 AM, you break the assumption. Correlated retries create synchronized storms that violate the smooth Poisson curve.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Thundering Herd" GitHub 2018

A database partition caused application workers to stall. When the database returned, every single worker retried their connection at the exact same millisecond. It wasn't a Poisson arrival rate; it was a synchronized, correlated surge that overwhelmed the connection pool.

Reference: GitHub's October 21 Incident Report
The "Midnight Cron" Scheduled Jobs

Every day at 00:00 UTC, 50,000 cron jobs fire simultaneously. The average load over 24 hours is perfectly fine. But the Poisson math breaks down because the arrivals are perfectly synchronized.

Reference: The Cron Job Problem (Jitter is mandatory)
The "Load Balancer Tick" Keep-Alive Synchronization

All HTTP keep-alive connections default to exactly 60 seconds. At second 60, every connection re-establishes simultaneously, creating a burst of TCP handshakes that looks nothing like a Poisson curve.

Reference: Brendan Gregg, "The Use of Performance Metrics"

2. The Exponential Distribution (Wait Times)

The Intuition: The Memoryless Bus

If Poisson models how many arrive, Exponential models how long you wait between them. It is the "memoryless" distribution: the chance of the next event happening in the next 10 seconds is exactly the same, regardless of how long you've already been waiting.

Your CPU doesn't "remember" that it has been processing a request for 50ms. At 51ms, the probability of it finishing in the next millisecond is identical to what it was at 1ms. This is why long-tail latencies are so brutal: the distribution has no memory of the fact that it's already ruined your SLA.

The Math

The probability density function for the time between arrivals, given a rate λ:

f(x) = λ · e-λx
Let's decode this:
  • λ: The rate of events (e.g., 2 events per second).
  • x: The time you are waiting (e.g., 0.5 seconds).
  • e: Euler's number.

Worked Example: If the average service rate is 100 requests per second (λ = 100), what is the probability that a specific request takes longer than 10 milliseconds (x = 0.01s)? The math says e^(-100 * 0.01) = e^-1, which is roughly 36.8%. That means over a third of your requests take longer than 10ms.

The SRE Trap

The exponential distribution assumes service times are random and memoryless. In reality, your system has cache hits (very fast) and cache misses (very slow). Real-world service times are Log-Normal (multiplicative compounding), not Exponential. If you size your queues using exponential math, the p99 tail will blindside you.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Timeout Illusion" gRPC Deadlines

You set a 200ms timeout. But because service times follow an exponential distribution, the tail can stretch out to infinity. The mean is 100ms, but 5% of requests take longer than 300ms. You didn't solve the queue; you just moved the error to the client.

Reference: gRPC Deadline Propagation
The "Cold Start" AWS Lambda

A Lambda function that usually runs in 50ms suddenly takes 2 seconds because of a cold start. The exponential distribution assumes the hazard rate is constant. Cold starts violate this by introducing a massive, heavy tail.

Reference: AWS Lambda Performance Optimization
The "GC Pause" JVM Stop-the-World

Your JVM serves requests in 5ms. But when the Garbage Collector runs, it stops the world for 500ms. The exponential memoryless property is broken by deterministic pauses.

Reference: Scott Oaks, "Java Performance: The Definitive Guide"

3. The Erlang Distribution (Pipelines)

The Intuition: The Assembly Line

Erlang models the sum of multiple sequential exponential stages. Think of an assembly line: Stage 1 takes a random amount of time, Stage 2 takes a random amount of time, etc. The total time is the sum of all stages.

This is your modern microservice architecture. A request hits the API Gateway, then the Auth service, then the User service, then the Database, then the Cache. Every hop adds variance. As you add more stages (higher Erlang k), the distribution becomes less "lumpy" and more tightly clustered around the mean. It becomes less chaotic but more predictable.

The Math

The probability density function for a sum of k exponential stages, each with rate λ:

f(x) = (λk · xk-1 · e-λx) / (k-1)!
Let's decode this:
  • k (Shape): The number of sequential stages in your pipeline.
  • λ: The service rate of each stage.
  • x: The total time to traverse all stages.

Worked Example: If you have a 3-stage pipeline (k=3), and each stage has a rate of 1 per second (λ=1), the mean time is 3 seconds. The Erlang distribution tells you that the probability of the whole pipeline taking exactly 3 seconds is roughly 27%. This is why multi-step checkout flows feel more predictable but take longer.

The SRE Trap

Erlang makes you think your pipeline is stable because the variance shrinks. But every stage you add to your architecture increases the mean latency. You can have a beautifully stable pipeline that is completely useless because the mean latency is 10 seconds. Little's Law (Concurrency = Throughput × Latency) dictates that you will need 10x more threads to maintain throughput.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Fan-Out Trap" Microservices

A single page load triggers 20 sequential microservice calls. Even if each call has a 99.9% success rate, the probability of the whole page loading successfully is 0.999^20 = 98%. And the Erlang mean latency is 20x that of a single service.

Reference: Dan Luu, "Latency Pitfalls"
The "Checkout Flow" Stripe / E-Commerce

A checkout involves: Cart validation → Inventory check → Payment authorization → Fraud check → Order creation. Five distinct Erlang stages. If you only test the final success rate, you miss the compound latency of the sequential pipeline.

Reference: Stripe Checkout Architecture
The "Retry Loop" Exponential Backoff

Every time a service fails and retries, it adds another Erlang stage to the request. A single request that retries 3 times has effectively traversed 4 sequential stages, each with its own exponential delay.

Reference: AWS Builders' Library: Timeouts, Retries, and Backoff with Jitter
The Core Problem: Kingman's Formula

All three distributions converge into Kingman's Formula, which describes the wait time in a heavily loaded queue. The formula states that as utilization approaches 100% (ρ → 1), the wait time approaches infinity. Shopify handles Black Friday by enforcing virtual waiting rooms—strict admission control that deliberately sheds load to prevent utilization from hitting that mathematical cliff.

The Lesson: You cannot buy your way out of a queue with more CPUs. You have to limit the number of people allowed in the line.
