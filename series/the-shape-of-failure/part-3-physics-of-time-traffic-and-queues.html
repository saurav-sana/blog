The Physics of Time, Traffic, and Queues

October 24, 2026 · Part 3 of 8

“Utilization is not a target. It is a limit.”

— Baron Schwartz, Systems Performance Author

Every quarter, a well-meaning FinOps spreadsheet warrior looks at an observability dashboard, sees an event streaming cluster running at 70% average CPU utilization, and declares victory by scaling down worker nodes. Two days later, CPU sits at a "healthy, cost-optimized" 91%, the Grafana gauges are still green, and your regulatory reporting pipeline is silently breaching compliance SLAs.

Why? Because capacity planning based on averages assumes traffic arrives like a Swiss train schedule and executes in deterministic lockstep. In reality, requests arrive in random clusters, execution times jitter wildly, and queues act as mathematical amplifiers for both. As utilization approaches 100%, waiting time doesn't grow linearly—it goes vertical.

To understand why a system can be simultaneously "healthy" on average and unusable in practice, model the arrival process (Poisson), service-time variability (Exponential as one baseline), sequential latency (Erlang), finite-queue overload, and the relationship between throughput, time, and concurrency (Little’s Law), alongside Kingman’s GI/G/1 waiting-time approximation. Each model has assumptions that must be tested against production telemetry.

1. The Poisson Distribution (Arrivals)

The Intuition: The Unscheduled Bus Stop

Imagine standing at a bus stop where buses arrive at an average rate of 4 per hour, completely independently of one another. You will not see a bus every 15 minutes on the dot. You will wait 40 minutes in the rain, and then three buses will show up bumper-to-bumper because randomness naturally clumps.

In distributed streaming systems, the Poisson distribution is a useful baseline for counts of arrivals when events occur independently, at a roughly stable average rate over the window being modelled. It is not a universal traffic law: coordinated clients, synchronized retries, scheduled jobs, and feedback loops can violate those assumptions. Use telemetry to test whether the model is defensible at the timescale that matters.

The probability of observing exactly $k$ arrivals in a fixed time window, given an average arrival rate of $\lambda$ events per window, is given by the Poisson Probability Mass Function (PMF):

$$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k \cdot e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$
Decoding the parameters:
  • $\lambda$ (Lambda): The expected (average) number of arrivals in your chosen time window. Crucially, for a Poisson distribution, the Variance also equals $\lambda$ ($\sigma^2 = \lambda$).
  • $k$: The exact integer number of requests arriving in that window ($k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).
  • $e$: Euler's constant ($\approx 2.71828$), the base of natural decay.
  • $k!$: Factorial of $k$ (the number of ways $k$ arrivals can permute).
Worked Scenario: The 100ms Micro-Burst & The 5-Slot Buffer

Consider a simplified admission model: a service sees an average of 20 requests per second, and we count arrivals in fixed, non-overlapping 100ms windows. For this calculation only, assume a window can admit at most five new arrivals and has no carry-over queue or service during the window. A real queue must also account for current occupancy, service completions, and arrivals across window boundaries; the Poisson count alone does not determine whether a real request is rejected.

How often do you drop traffic when your server is "only 20% utilized"? Let's do the math for a 100ms ($0.1\text{s}$) window:

Window Rate: $\lambda = 20 \text{ req/s} \times 0.1\text{s} = 2.0$ arrivals per 100ms
Base Decay: $e^{-2} \approx 0.13534$

$P(X = 0) = \frac{2^0 \cdot 0.13534}{0!} = 0.1353$ (13.53% — dead quiet)
$P(X = 1) = \frac{2^1 \cdot 0.13534}{1!} = 0.2707$ (27.07%)
$P(X = 2) = \frac{2^2 \cdot 0.13534}{2!} = 0.2707$ (27.07% — the average)
$P(X = 3) = \frac{2^3 \cdot 0.13534}{3!} = 0.1804$ (18.04%)
$P(X = 4) = \frac{2^4 \cdot 0.13534}{4!} = 0.0902$ (9.02%)
$P(X = 5) = \frac{2^5 \cdot 0.13534}{5!} = 0.0361$ (3.61% — queue full)
------------------------------------------------------------
Cumulative Safe Probability: $P(X \le 5) = 0.9834$ (98.34%)
Overflow Probability: $P(X \ge 6) = 1 - 0.9834 = 0.0166$ (1.66%)

The Punchline: Under this Poisson model, 1.66% of 100ms windows contain six or more arrivals. There are 600 such windows in a minute, so that is about 10 overflow-sized windows per minute on average under the toy admission rule—not a prediction of exactly ten rejected requests. Actual rejection rates depend on queue occupancy, service completions, and how admission is implemented. A green minute-level CPU average can still hide short bursts.

Telemetry Check: The Dispersion Index ($D = \sigma^2 / \mu$)

For Poisson counts, the Dispersion Index (variance-to-mean ratio) is 1.0. A value materially above 1 suggests overdispersion at the measured window size, but there is no universal cutoff such as 1.5 that automatically invalidates a model. Measure several window sizes, inspect autocorrelation and burst duration, and compare candidate models against held-out telemetry before sizing buffers. Overdispersion is a diagnostic clue—not proof of one particular distribution or cause.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "43-Second Glitch, 24-Hour Hangover" GitHub 2018

On October 21, 2018, a brief network partition between GitHub data-center facilities triggered a chain of database failover and recovery problems. Although the network disruption itself was brief, restoring a consistent and healthy service state took much longer; GitHub’s post-incident analysis describes the prolonged recovery and the operational consequences. The lesson is not that every short partition creates a long outage, but that failover, backlog, replication, and recovery procedures can dominate the duration of an incident.

Reference: GitHub's October 21 Post-Incident Analysis
The Self-Inflicted DDoS (`0 0 * * *`) Scheduled Jobs

Synthetic example: 50,000 tenants schedule a daily report at midnight. The day-wide average is only $50{,}000/86{,}400 \approx 0.58$ jobs/s, but synchronized scheduling can create a large burst at the top of the hour. Spreading starts uniformly across 15 minutes reduces the average launch rate during that window to about $50{,}000/900 \approx 55.6$ jobs/s, before accounting for retries or variable job duration. Jitter reduces synchronization; it does not guarantee a Poisson process. Combine it with concurrency limits, queueing, and rate-aware admission.

Reference: AWS Builders' Library: Adding Jitter to Scheduled Workloads

Finite Queues: When Arrival Rate Exceeds Capacity

Probability models describe variability; a capacity balance explains whether backlog can remain bounded. If the sustained arrival rate exceeds the sustained service rate, a finite queue eventually fills. This is a fluid approximation that ignores short-term randomness, but it is useful for first-order overload planning.

Worked Scenario: A Bounded Queue Fills in Ten Seconds

A consumer receives 120 requests/s, can complete 100 requests/s, and has 200 waiting slots. Start with an empty queue and assume constant rates, no drops, and no additional scaling.

Net backlog growth = arrival rate − service rate
$120 - 100 = 20$ requests/s

Time to fill = queue capacity / net growth
$200 / 20 = \mathbf{10\text{ seconds}}$

Once the queue fills, the system must reject, shed, or defer excess work; otherwise memory or latency can grow without bound elsewhere. This calculation is a fluid baseline, not an exact prediction under bursty arrivals or variable service times.

Engineering controls: bound queue length and queued age, propagate deadlines, reject work that cannot finish within its remaining budget, shed low-priority work, apply per-tenant limits, and scale only when added capacity can arrive before the queue fills. After the burst, drain rate is service capacity minus new arrivals; if arrivals remain at 120/s while capacity is 100/s, the backlog never drains.

2. The Exponential Distribution (Service Times)

The Intuition: The Memoryless Curse

If arrivals follow a Poisson process, the time gap between arrivals follows an Exponential distribution. Queueing theorists also love using it as the baseline model for service time (how long a worker takes to process one request) in an $M/M/1$ queue.

Its defining quirk is the memoryless property: the coin has no memory of past flips. In an exponential world, if a request has already been running for 50ms, the probability that it needs another 20ms is identical to a brand-new request needing 20ms. The universe does not owe your query a completion just because it has already wasted your CPU cycles.

For a service or arrival process with rate $\mu$ (where mean service time is $1/\mu$), the Probability Density Function (PDF) and the Tail Survival Function are:

$$f(t) = \mu \cdot e^{-\mu t} \quad \Big| \quad P(T > t) = e^{-\mu t}$$
Decoding the parameters:
  • $\mu$ (Service Rate): The completion rate (e.g., $\mu = 100\text{ req/sec}$, meaning mean service time $E[T] = 1/\mu = 10\text{ms}$).
  • $t$: The elapsed time threshold you are evaluating ($t \ge 0$).
  • $P(T > t) = e^{-\mu t}$: The Survival Function—the exact probability that a request takes longer than time $t$ and blows past your deadline.
Worked Scenario: The 30ms gRPC Deadline Assassination

Your team builds a pricing microservice with an average execution time of 10ms ($\mu = 100\text{ req/s} = 0.1\text{ req/ms}$). During an architecture review, someone proposes setting a strict upstream gRPC timeout of 30ms, arguing: "30ms is 3x the average latency! Only broken requests take 3x the average!"

Assuming exponentially distributed service times, let's see what happens when you deploy that timeout:

Mean Service Time: $E[T] = \frac{1}{\mu} = 10\text{ms}$ ($\mu = 0.1\text{ ms}^{-1}$)

1. How many requests take longer than the 10ms "average"?
$P(T > 10\text{ms}) = e^{-(0.1 \times 10)} = e^{-1} \approx \mathbf{36.79\%}$
(Note: Median latency is $\frac{\ln(2)}{\mu} = 6.93\text{ms}$. Most requests are fast, a long tail pulls the mean up.)

2. How many requests exceed the 30ms gRPC deadline?
$P(T > 30\text{ms}) = e^{-(0.1 \times 30)} = e^{-3} \approx \mathbf{0.0498\ (4.98\%)}$

3. Where is the actual p99 latency?
$0.01 = e^{-0.1 \times t} \implies t = \frac{-\ln(0.01)}{0.1} = \frac{4.605}{0.1} = \mathbf{46.05\text{ms}}$

The Punchline: In this exponential service-time model, a 30ms threshold is exceeded by about 4.98% of requests. This is a modelled service-time exceedance, not automatically the end-to-end gRPC timeout rate: queueing, network transit, and downstream work also consume the deadline. Propagate deadlines, cancel work when callers abandon it, and use bounded retries with backoff and jitter so timed-out work does not multiply load.

Telemetry Check: Bimodal Mixture Reality ($c_s^2$)

The Exponential distribution has a Coefficient of Variation ($c_s = \sigma / \mu$) of exactly 1.0. Real production services feature bimodal distributions: 90% cache hits at 1ms, and 10% cache misses hitting Postgres at 80ms ($c_s \gg 1$). Furthermore, Stop-the-World garbage collection pauses violate independence by freezing all worker threads simultaneously. Never treat service times as purely exponential if your telemetry exhibits heavy-tailed Pareto spikes.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Bimodal Cold Start" AWS Lambda

Serverless functions don't follow a single exponential curve—they are a mixture model. Warm invocations resolve in 15ms, while cold starts pulling a bloated container image or initializing a heavy DI framework take 1,800ms. If you alert on average latency, a 1% cold-start rate hides effortlessly inside the mean while ruining your p99 SLO.

Reference: AWS Lambda Performance Optimization
The "Stop-the-World Guillotine" JVM GC Pauses

Exponential models assume every request draws an independent random ticket. A Stop-the-World Garbage Collection pause violates independence by freezing all 200 worker threads simultaneously for 400ms. When the JVM wakes up, every in-flight request has breached its SLA simultaneously, and the ingress queue is packed with angry retries.

Reference: Scott Oaks, "Java Performance: The Definitive Guide"

3. The Erlang Distribution (Sequential Pipelines)

The Intuition: The Microservice Relay Race

What happens when a single request has to pass sequentially through Ingress → Schema Validation → Enrichment → Partition Router → Storage Write before returning an acknowledgement? If each of those $k$ stages has an independent, identically distributed exponential duration with rate $\lambda$, and the request waits for all stages sequentially, the sum of stage durations follows an Erlang-$k$ distribution. Real stages may be correlated, have different rates, overlap, or contain queueing; validate these assumptions before using the curve as a production predictor.

Unlike the Exponential curve (which peaks at $t = 0$), the Erlang distribution has a hump in the middle. You can no longer get lucky and finish in $0.1\text{ms}$, because the request has to survive $k$ consecutive hops. Near-zero latencies become less likely, the mean multiplies by $k$, and variances add across independent stages ($\sigma_{\text{total}}^2 = k / \lambda^2$). This is a latency-sum model; it is not the product of stage success probabilities and does not by itself model end-to-end reliability.

The Probability Density Function (PDF) and Tail Survival Function for the sum of $k$ independent exponential stages, each with the same rate $\lambda$, are:

$$f(x) = \frac{\lambda^k \cdot x^{k-1} \cdot e^{-\lambda x}}{(k-1)!} \quad \Big| \quad P(T > x) = e^{-\lambda x} \sum_{n=0}^{k-1} \frac{(\lambda x)^n}{n!}$$
Decoding the parameters:
  • $k$ (Shape): The integer number of sequential hops or pipeline stages ($k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$).
  • $\lambda$ (Rate): The service rate of each individual stage. Mean $E[T] = k / \lambda$.
  • Why $P(T > x)$ is a sum: A pipeline takes longer than time $x$ if and only if strictly fewer than $k$ stages have completed by time $x$.
Worked Scenario: The 3-Hop Pipeline vs. The 50ms API Gateway SLA

Your API Gateway enforces an end-to-end SLA of 50ms. To fulfill a request, your orchestrator calls 3 sequential microservices ($k = 3$: Auth, Profile, Billing). Each service takes an average of 10ms ($\lambda = 0.1\text{ ms}^{-1}$). The expected total latency is $E[T] = 3 / 0.1 = 30\text{ms}$.

What percentage of user requests breach the 50ms SLA ($x = 50\text{ms}$)? Plugging $\lambda x = 5$ and $k = 3$ into the Erlang Survival formula:

Dimensionless Budget: $\lambda x = 0.1 \text{ ms}^{-1} \times 50\text{ms} = 5.0$
Base Decay: $e^{-5} \approx 0.006738$

Sum terms for $n = 0$ to $k - 1$ ($n = 0, 1, 2$ stages completed by 50ms):
$n = 0$: $\frac{5^0}{0!} = 1.0$
$n = 1$: $\frac{5^1}{1!} = 5.0$
$n = 2$: $\frac{5^2}{2!} = \frac{25}{2} = 12.5$
------------------------------------------------------------
Series Sum = $1.0 + 5.0 + 12.5 = 18.5$

SLA Breach Probability: $P(T > 50\text{ms}) = 0.006738 \times 18.5 = 0.12465$ (12.47%)

The Punchline: Even though each individual microservice averages a snappy 10ms and the pipeline averages 30ms, 12.47% of your users—roughly 1 in 8 requests—will fail the 50ms SLA because variances add up across sequential hops.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Death by 20 Paper Cuts" Microservice Chains

When an architecture diagram looks like the London Underground and a single request traverses 20 sequential microservice hops, each boasting "three nines" ($99.9\%$) availability, end-to-end reliability plummets to $0.999^{20} = 98.02\%$. Worse, every hop adds network serialization, Envoy proxy traversal, and queueing jitter—turning fast local functions into a slow Erlang march.

Reference: Dan Luu, "Latency Pitfalls in Distributed Systems"
The "Synchronous Checkout Gauntlet" Stripe / E-Commerce

A naive checkout endpoint runs Cart Validation → Tax Calculation → Fraud Scoring → Card Auth → Ledger Write → Email Receipt synchronously in the user's request path ($k = 6$). High-scale payment architectures ruthlessly collapse synchronous stages—executing Fraud and Tax in parallel or pushing Ledger/Receipts onto asynchronous durable queues—to shrink $k$ on the critical path.

Reference: Stripe Engineering: Designing Robust Payment APIs
The Grand Unification: Little’s Law, Kingman’s Approximation & The 91% Utilization Trap

Consider a hypothetical single serial processing lane—for example, one hot partition or one effectively single-server queue in a financial trade-ingestion path. This is intentionally not a prediction for an entire multi-worker AKS cluster. The example shows how variability and high utilization can amplify waiting time when a GI/G/1 approximation is appropriate.

Assume the lane receives 50 events/s and can complete 55 events/s. Its mean service time is therefore $E[S]=1/55\text{ s}\approx18.18\text{ ms}$ and utilization is $\rho=50/55\approx90.9\%$. For the illustrative calculation, assume squared coefficients of variation $c_a^2=3.5$ and $c_s^2=4.2$.

Kingman’s GI/G/1 approximation estimates the mean queueing delay as:

$$E[W_q] \approx \left( \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} \right) \cdot \left( \frac{c_a^2+c_s^2}{2} \right) \cdot E[S]$$
  • Utilization multiplier: $0.909/(1-0.909)\approx10.0$
  • Variability multiplier: $(3.5+4.2)/2=3.85$
  • Approximate mean queue wait: $10.0\times3.85\times18.18\text{ ms}\approx700\text{ ms}$
  • Approximate mean time in system: $700+18.18\approx718\text{ ms}$

Interpretation: in this illustrative single-server model, a lane at about 91% utilization can have a mean wait that overwhelms a 200ms processing objective. The inputs are hypothetical, and Kingman’s formula is an approximation for a stable GI/G/1 queue—not a direct predictor for an arbitrary multi-server cluster. For a multi-worker service, model the actual topology and validate against trace/queue telemetry or discrete-event simulation.

Little’s Law ($L=\lambda W$): if the lane is stable and its long-run throughput is 50 events/s, a mean time in system of 0.718s implies:

$$L=50\text{ events/s}\times0.718\text{ s}\approx35.9\text{ events in the system on average}$$

This is the average number of events in the defined system boundary—not 36 open database connections, Cosmos DB operations, or transactions by definition. Those quantities require separate measurements and a resource model. A downstream RU-throttling event and an optimistic-concurrency conflict are different failure mechanisms; do not infer either from Little’s Law alone. If downstream latency rises, capacity may fall and utilization may exceed 100%, at which point the queue is unstable unless admission or capacity changes.

Engineering controls: measure arrival rate, completion rate, queue depth and age, service-time distribution, and per-stage latency; keep utilization headroom; enforce bounded queues and deadline-aware admission; apply retry budgets with exponential backoff and jitter; separate hot partitions/tenants; and validate tail latency under representative burst and payload distributions. Treat the model as a hypothesis to test, not as proof of a production incident.
