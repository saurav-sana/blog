The Physics of Time, Traffic, and Queues

October 24, 2026 · Part 3 of 8

“Utilization is not a target. It is a limit.”

— Baron Schwartz, Systems Performance Author

Queueing theory explains why a service can look healthy while users experience painful latency. In a simple stable single-server queue, as utilization approaches 100% while arrival and service variability remain nonzero, expected waiting time can grow sharply and the idealized model diverges as utilization approaches one. Real systems have finite queues, timeouts, admission controls, multiple servers, and changing workloads—so the result is not “infinite latency” in production, but often rising tail latency, queue growth, and dropped or timed-out work.

To understand why, we have to model the three distinct physical mechanics of any queue: how things arrive, how long they take to serve, and what happens when you chain them together.

1. The Poisson Distribution (Arrivals)

The Intuition: The Bus Stop

A Poisson process is a useful model for arrivals that occur independently at a roughly constant average rate. Think of a bus stop where arrival times are not scheduled. It can produce quiet intervals and short bursts, but it does not model every kind of burst: scheduled jobs, retries, promotions, and other coordinated traffic can be much more correlated.

In distributed systems, this can be a first approximation for traffic reaching a load balancer. The model assumes independent increments and a constant rate over the interval being modeled. Real traffic often changes by time of day and can be correlated across users or services. Capacity planning should therefore examine measured burstiness and time windows—not just the daily average or a Poisson fit.

The Math

The probability of observing exactly k arrivals in a fixed time window, given an average arrival rate of λ:

P(X = k) = (λk · e-λ) / k!
Let's decode this:
  • λ (Lambda): The average arrival rate (e.g., 50 requests per second).
  • k: The actual number of requests you are calculating the probability for.
  • e: Euler's number (approx 2.718).
  • k!: Factorial (e.g., 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24).

Worked Example: If your average rate is 10 requests/sec (λ = 10), what is the chance of getting exactly 15 requests in one second? The formula gives roughly 3.4%. For λ = 10, the probability of exactly 30 arrivals in one second is about 0.000017% (approximately 1.7 × 10⁻⁷), under the Poisson assumptions. Do not interpret a rare single-window event as automatically becoming common because there are many servers: aggregate risk depends on the number of windows, dependence between sources, and whether the model fits the observed traffic. Synchronized clients can create bursts for a different reason—the arrivals are correlated, so the independent-arrival model is the wrong model.

The SRE Trap

A homogeneous Poisson process assumes independent increments and a constant rate; it is not a universal model of production traffic. Retries after a shared outage and cron jobs scheduled for the same instant can synchronize arrivals. Use jitter, backoff, request coalescing, and admission control to reduce that correlation—and validate the arrival model against telemetry.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Thundering Herd" GitHub 2018

GitHub’s October 21, 2018 incident was triggered by a 43-second network partition during routine network maintenance. Automated database failover created divergent database states across regions, and the resulting unsupported topology contributed to prolonged service degradation. It is a valuable example of a short disturbance producing a long recovery—but GitHub’s published postmortem does not describe the incident as every worker retrying at the same millisecond or as a connection-pool thundering herd.

Reference: GitHub's October 21 Incident Report
The "Midnight Cron" Scheduled Jobs

Imagine 50,000 scheduled jobs configured for the same minute. The daily average can look harmless while the scheduled arrival rate is sharply concentrated. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a measured claim about a particular fleet. Randomize schedules with jitter and enforce concurrency limits.

Reference: The Cron Job Problem (Jitter is mandatory)
The "Load Balancer Tick" Keep-Alive Synchronization

Connection lifetimes and keep-alive policies vary by client, proxy, load balancer, and server; there is no universal 60-second default. However, synchronized connection expiry or coordinated health checks can create handshake bursts. Add bounded connection churn and jitter where appropriate, and inspect actual connection-lifetime settings before diagnosing this pattern.

Reference: Brendan Gregg, "The Use of Performance Metrics"

2. The Exponential Distribution (Wait Times)

The Intuition: The Memoryless Bus

For a homogeneous Poisson process, the inter-arrival times follow an exponential distribution. It is memoryless: conditional on an event not having happened yet, the distribution of the additional wait is the same as it was at the start. This is a property of the model—not a rule that every production service or CPU follows.

If a request’s service time really were exponential, its remaining service time would be memoryless. Real request durations often are not: work size, cache state, downstream calls, scheduling, and pauses all matter. The exponential model is useful when its assumptions fit; it should not be used to explain every latency tail.

The Math

The probability density function for an exponential random variable with rate λ (units: 1/time) is:

f(x) = λ · e-λx
Let's decode this:
  • λ: The rate of events (e.g., 2 events per second).
  • x: The time you are waiting (e.g., 0.5 seconds).
  • e: Euler's number.

Worked Example: If the average service rate is 100 requests per second (λ = 100), what is the probability that a specific request takes longer than 10 milliseconds (x = 0.01s)? The math says e^(-100 * 0.01) = e^-1, which is roughly 36.8%. That means over a third of your requests take longer than 10ms.

The SRE Trap

An exponential service-time model assumes a constant hazard rate. Real service times can be mixtures (for example, cache hits and cache misses), log-normal-like, heavy-tailed, multimodal, or shaped by queues and pauses. There is no single distribution that describes all production services. Fit candidate models to representative telemetry, validate tail quantiles, and use the model that matches the workload and decision.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Timeout Illusion" gRPC Deadlines

Suppose service time is exponential with mean 100 ms (rate 10/s). Then P(T > 200 ms) = e⁻² ≈ 13.5%, and P(T > 300 ms) = e⁻³ ≈ 5.0%. A 200 ms deadline can bound how long the caller waits, but it does not by itself cancel downstream work or remove queued load. Propagate deadlines, cancel work where safe, and use bounded queues and concurrency limits.

Reference: gRPC Deadline Propagation
The "Cold Start" AWS Lambda

A serverless invocation may have a fast warm path and a slower initialization path. That mixture can create a multimodal latency distribution; it is not necessarily an exponential tail. Measure cold-start frequency and duration separately, then decide whether provisioned concurrency, initialization changes, or a looser SLO is justified.

Reference: AWS Lambda Performance Optimization
The "GC Pause" JVM Stop-the-World

A stop-the-world garbage-collection pause can add a shared delay to many requests, producing correlated latency spikes rather than independent exponential service times. Actual pause behavior depends on the collector, heap, workload, and configuration; measure pause distributions and consider low-pause collectors only after profiling.

Reference: Scott Oaks, "Java Performance: The Definitive Guide"

3. The Erlang Distribution (Pipelines)

The Intuition: The Assembly Line

The Erlang distribution models the sum of k independent exponential stages that share the same rate λ. It is a special case of the gamma distribution. It can be a useful pipeline model when each stage’s duration is approximately exponential and independent; real microservice hops often violate one or both assumptions.

A sequential request path can be approximated as a sum of stage durations. Under the Erlang assumptions, increasing k while holding each stage rate fixed increases the mean total time (k/λ) and reduces the coefficient of variation (1/√k). If instead you hold the total mean fixed while increasing the number of equal stages, the sum becomes more concentrated relative to that mean. This mathematical result does not mean adding real services automatically makes a system more predictable: shared dependencies, queueing, retries, and correlated pauses can dominate.

The Math

The probability density for a sum of k independent exponential stages with the same rate λ (a special case of the gamma distribution) is:

f(x) = (λk · xk-1 · e-λx) / (k-1)!
Let's decode this:
  • k (Shape): The number of sequential stages in your pipeline.
  • λ: The service rate of each stage.
  • x: The total time to traverse all stages.

Worked Example: If you have a 3-stage pipeline (k=3), and each stage has a rate of 1 per second (λ=1), the mean time is 3 seconds. For k = 3 and λ = 1 s⁻¹, the mean total time is 3 seconds. Because this is a continuous distribution, the probability of taking exactly 3.000… seconds is zero; the density at x = 3 seconds is 9e⁻³/2 ≈ 0.224 per second. A probability requires an interval, such as P(2.5 s ≤ T ≤ 3.5 s).

The SRE Trap

Little’s Law states L = λW for a stable system, where L is the long-run average number of items in the system, λ is throughput, and W is average time in the system. If throughput stays constant while average latency rises tenfold, average in-flight concurrency also rises tenfold. That does not automatically mean ten times as many threads are needed: async I/O, CPU-bound work, queueing, and per-request resource use change the implementation implications.

Scenarios & Case Studies

The "Dependency-Chain Trap" Microservices

If a request requires 20 sequential dependencies, each succeeds with probability 0.999, and failures are independent, the probability all 20 succeed is 0.999²⁰ ≈ 98.0%. This is a dependency-chain example, not fan-out. Total latency is the sum of stage latencies, but it follows an Erlang distribution only if the stages are independent exponentials with a common rate.

Reference: Dan Luu, "Latency Pitfalls"
The "Checkout Flow" Stripe / E-Commerce

A checkout may include cart validation, inventory checks, payment authorization, fraud checks, and order creation. These are five conceptual stages, not automatically five Erlang stages: the Erlang model requires independent exponential durations with the same rate. Measure each stage and the end-to-end path, including queue time, retries, and dependency failures.

Reference: Stripe Checkout Architecture
The "Retry Loop" Exponential Backoff

Retries can add extra attempts and backoff time to a request, but they do not automatically create Erlang-distributed stages. Retry paths are conditional on failure, often correlated with overload, and can amplify the load on a struggling dependency. Use bounded retries, exponential backoff with jitter, retry budgets, and cancellation when the caller no longer needs the result.

Reference: AWS Builders' Library: Timeouts, Retries, and Backoff with Jitter
Queueing Theory: Kingman’s Approximation

For a stable GI/G/1 queue (general inter-arrival and service-time distributions, one server), a commonly used approximation is:

E[Wq] ≈ (ρ / (1 − ρ)) · ((ca2 + cs2) / 2) · E[S]

Here ρ is utilization, ca is the coefficient of variation of inter-arrival times, cs is the coefficient of variation of service times, and E[S] is mean service time. It is an approximation, not a universal law for every multi-server architecture.

The Core Problem: Kingman's Formula

A useful bridge from arrival and service variability to queueing delay is Kingman’s approximation for a stable GI/G/1 queue: E[Wq] ≈ (ρ/(1−ρ)) × ((ca² + cs²)/2) × E[S], where ρ is utilization, ca is the coefficient of variation of inter-arrival times, cs is the coefficient of variation of service times, and E[S] is mean service time. It is an approximation under specific single-server assumptions—not a formula that all distributions “converge into.” With variability held fixed, the utilization multiplier is 19 at ρ = 0.95 and 99 at ρ = 0.99. Real multi-server systems need an appropriate model or measurements. Virtual waiting rooms and admission control can protect capacity, but avoid attributing a specific strategy to a company without a source.

The lesson: More capacity can reduce utilization and queueing delay, but it is not a substitute for controlling overload. Bound queues, apply admission control, shed or defer noncritical work, and use autoscaling with enough headroom for its reaction time.
