The Physics of Time, Traffic, and Queues

October 24, 2026 · Part 3 of 8

“Utilization is not a target. It is a limit.”

— Architecture field note

Consider a recurring capacity-planning mistake. An event-streaming cluster averages 70% CPU utilization, so a cost review recommends removing workers. After the change, utilization spends more time near 90%, and a reporting pipeline begins missing its processing objective. These percentages are illustrative, not universal capacity thresholds; CPU utilization alone does not reveal per-partition saturation, service-time variability, queue age, or the capacity available for recovery.

The mechanism is queueing. Arrivals do not line up perfectly with service completions, and service times are not identical. When a single-server queue operates close to its service capacity, variability leaves less idle time in which to absorb bursts. Waiting time can then rise sharply even while average throughput still matches average demand. The exact response depends on arrival variability, service-time variability, scheduling, buffering, and whether capacity can be added quickly.

This chapter moves from arrival counts to inter-arrival and service times, then to sequential stage durations, finite and multi-server queues, Little’s Law, and Kingman’s mean-wait approximation for a single-server \(GI/G/1\) queue. These are deliberately different models. A model is useful only when its assumptions match the workload and the operational decision being made.

1. The Poisson Distribution (Arrivals)

The Intuition: The Unscheduled Bus Stop

Imagine standing at a bus stop where buses arrive at an average rate of 4 per hour, completely independently of one another. You will not see a bus every 15 minutes on the dot. You will wait 40 minutes in the rain, and then three buses will show up bumper-to-bumper because randomness naturally clumps.

In distributed streaming systems, the Poisson distribution is a useful baseline for counts of arrivals when events occur independently, at a roughly stable average rate over the window being modelled. It is not a universal traffic law: coordinated clients, synchronized retries, scheduled jobs, and feedback loops can violate those assumptions. Use telemetry to test whether the model is defensible at the timescale that matters.

Let \(\lambda\) be the arrival rate, measured in arrivals per unit time (for example, queries/second), and let \(t\) be the observation-window duration in that same time unit. The homogeneous Poisson counting process is \(N(t) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t)\). The product \(\lambda t\) is dimensionless and is the expected number of arrivals in the window—not an arrival rate. For a homogeneous Poisson process, inter-arrival times are independent exponential random variables with rate \(\lambda\), so their mean is \(1/\lambda\).

The model assumes independent increments and a constant rate over the window being analysed; it also assigns probability zero to exactly simultaneous arrivals. The exponential inter-arrival distribution is memoryless. These assumptions can fail with coordinated clients, batching, scheduled work, rate limits, and feedback-driven traffic.

The probability of observing exactly \(k\) arrivals in a fixed interval of duration \(t\) is given by the Poisson probability mass function (PMF):

\[ P(N(t) = k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k \cdot e^{-\lambda t}}{k!} \]
Decoding the parameters:
  • \(\lambda\): Arrival rate, in arrivals per unit time. \(t\): Window duration in the same time unit. \(\lambda t\): Expected arrival count in that window; for a Poisson count, the variance also equals this mean.
  • \(k\): The exact non-negative integer number of arrivals in that window (\(k=0,1,2,\ldots\)); the observation duration satisfies \(t\ge0\).
  • \(e\): Euler's number (\(\approx 2.71828\)), the base of natural decay.
  • \(k!\): Factorial of \(k\) (the number of ways \(k\) arrivals can permute).

For example, with \(\lambda = 10\) arrivals/second and \(t = 1\) second, the expected count is \(\lambda t = 10\). The probability of exactly 30 arrivals is \(e^{-10}10^{30}/30! \approx 1.71 \times 10^{-7}\), or about \(0.0000171\%\). An unusual count in one interval does not by itself establish overload: service capacity, existing queue occupancy, and the interval boundary determine whether backlog accumulates.

Worked Scenario: The Analytics Gateway & The 100ms Micro-Burst

Consider a simplified admission controller for our Multi-Tenant Analytics Gateway: the service sees an average of 20 queries per second across all tenants, and we count arrivals in fixed, non-overlapping 100ms windows. For this discrete calculation only, assume a window can admit at most five new queries and has no carry-over queue or background service draining queries mid-window. (A real continuous queue must also account for current occupancy and async service completions; the Poisson discrete count alone does not determine whether a real request is rejected).

The mean offered arrival rate is 20 queries/s, one fifth of the stated nominal processing rate of 100 queries/s. That average-rate comparison is not a guarantee about any 100ms interval. For this deliberately simplified admission rule, count how often more than five arrivals appear in a window; the five-query cap is a per-window admission limit, not a derivation of the worker pool's service capacity:

Expected arrivals in the window: \(\lambda t = 20 \text{ queries/s} \times 0.1\text{ s} = 2.0\) arrivals per 100ms
Base Decay: \(e^{-2} \approx 0.13534\)

\(P(N(0.1) = 0) = \frac{2.0^0 \cdot 0.13534}{0!} = 0.1353\) (13.53% — dead quiet)
\(P(N(0.1) = 1) = \frac{2.0^1 \cdot 0.13534}{1!} = 0.2707\) (27.07%)
\(P(N(0.1) = 2) = \frac{2.0^2 \cdot 0.13534}{2!} = 0.2707\) (27.07% — the average)
\(P(N(0.1) = 3) = \frac{2.0^3 \cdot 0.13534}{3!} = 0.1804\) (18.04%)
\(P(N(0.1) = 4) = \frac{2.0^4 \cdot 0.13534}{4!} = 0.0902\) (9.02%)
\(P(N(0.1) = 5) = \frac{2.0^5 \cdot 0.13534}{5!} = 0.0361\) (3.61% — admission cap reached)
------------------------------------------------------------
Cumulative Probability: \(P(N(0.1) \le 5) = 0.983436\) (98.34%)
Window-overflow Probability: \(P(N(0.1) \ge 6) = 1 - 0.983436 = 0.016564\) (1.66%)

Interpretation: Under the stated model, 1.66% of non-overlapping 100ms windows contain at least six arrivals. Across 600 windows per minute, the expected number of windows exceeding the five-arrival admission cap is \(600 \times 0.016564 \approx 9.94\) per minute. This is an expected count of overflow-sized windows, not a prediction of the number of dropped requests: the excess requests per window vary. The calculation also assumes a homogeneous Poisson process; real burst correlation can change the result substantially.

Telemetry Check: The Dispersion Index (\(I = \text{Var}(N(t)) / \mathbb{E}[N(t)]\))

For Poisson counts, the Index of Dispersion (variance-to-mean ratio) is exactly 1.0. Real production traffic is frequently burstier. A value materially above 1 (\(I > 1\)) indicates overdispersion, meaning arrivals are clumpier than random chance allows—often caused by cron-aligned batch bursts or synchronized client retries. There is no universal cutoff that automatically invalidates the model, but overdispersion is a diagnostic clue that you must measure several window sizes and inspect autocorrelation before sizing buffers.

Finite Queues: When Arrival Rate Exceeds Capacity

Probability describes arrival variability; queueing balance determines whether work accumulates. In an infinite-buffer \(M/M/1\) queue, arrivals are Poisson at rate \(\lambda\), one server has exponential service rate \(\mu\), and the first-come, first-served queue is stable only when \(\rho = \lambda/\mu < 1\). Here \(\mu\) is measured in completions per unit time and the mean service time is \(1/\mu\). For this model, the probability an arrival must wait is \(P(\text{wait})=\rho\) (by PASTA), the mean number in the system is \(L=\rho/(1-\rho)\), and the mean queue length is \(L_q=\rho^2/(1-\rho)\). Little’s Law then gives \(W=1/(\mu-\lambda)\) and \(W_q=\rho/(\mu-\lambda)\). These are steady-state means, not burst-by-burst predictions.

The preceding 200-waiting-slot worker-pool example is an aggregate fluid balance, not the single-server model below. A finite-buffer \(M/M/1/K\) queue uses a different admission contract: \(K\) counts all places in the system, including the one request in service. An arrival finding \(K\) requests already present is blocked. For \(\rho=\lambda/\mu\ne1\), the steady-state probability of state \(n\) is \(p_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n/(1-\rho^{K+1})\), for \(n=0,\ldots,K\); the blocking probability is \(P_K=p_K\). At \(\rho=1\), all \(K+1\) states have probability \(1/(K+1)\), so the blocking probability is also \(1/(K+1)\). The effective admitted throughput is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}=\lambda(1-P_K)\). A finite-state loss system has a stationary distribution even when \(\lambda\ge\mu\), but it pays for overload through blocking rather than unbounded queue growth.

Do not confuse the textbook notation with an implementation-specific buffer setting. If an application allows \(K_q\) waiting requests plus one request in service, its total system capacity is \(K=K_q+1\) in the single-server \(M/M/1/K\) notation. A full queue may reject immediately, drop according to a policy, or apply backpressure upstream; those are different admission behaviours. A rejected request does not accrue local queue wait, but it may still experience client-side delay, retry, or upstream buffering.

Worked Scenario: Consumer Lag in the Analytics Gateway

An Azure Event Hubs consumer group feeding the Analytics Gateway receives a sustained aggregate 120 events/s during a market-open burst. Its assigned workers can process an aggregate 100 events/s. The application buffer has a strict limit of 200 waiting slots, in addition to work already executing. Start with an empty waiting queue and assume constant aggregate rates, no drops before the buffer fills, and no auto-scaling. This is a fluid backlog calculation for a worker pool—not an exact \(M/M/1/K\) prediction.

Net backlog growth = arrival rate − service rate
\(120 - 100 = 20\) events/s

Time to fill = queue capacity / net growth
\(t_{\mathrm{fill}} = \frac{200}{120 - 100} = \mathbf{10\text{ seconds}}\)

The fluid estimate reaches the 200-slot waiting limit after \(200/20=10\) seconds. At that point, the implementation must enforce its admission policy: reject new work, drop according to an explicit policy, or backpressure the producer. The formula does not determine which policy is used, nor the exact probability of blocking under bursty arrivals and variable service times.

Recovery matters as much as saturation. If the burst subsides to 60 events/s while processing capacity remains 100 events/s, the net drain rate is \(100-60=40\) events/s. A 200-event backlog then takes approximately \(200/40=5\) seconds to clear under the same fluid assumptions. If arrivals remain at 120/s, the backlog never drains because demand still exceeds capacity. Bound queue length and age, propagate deadlines, reject work that cannot finish within its remaining budget, shed low-priority work, and scale only if added capacity arrives before the buffer fills. A larger queue buys time; it does not create processing capacity.

Partition-level check: the 120 and 100 events/s figures above are aggregate rates. For a partitioned stream, also measure each partition's \(\lambda_i\), its assigned processing capacity \(\mu_i\), and lag/queue age. Aggregate headroom can coexist with one hot partition whose arrival rate exceeds its effective service rate. Consumer parallelism is constrained by partition ownership and the number of active consumers; adding workers cannot make one serially processed partition run concurrently unless the processing design changes.

2. The Exponential Distribution (Service Times)

The Intuition: The Memoryless Curse

If arrivals follow a Poisson process, the time gap between arrivals follows an Exponential distribution. Queueing theorists also love using it as the baseline model for service time (how long a single server takes to process one request) in an \(M/M/1\) queue.

Its defining quirk is the memoryless property: the coin has no memory of past flips. In an exponential world, if a request has already been running for 50ms, the probability that it needs another 20ms is identical to a brand-new request needing 20ms. The universe does not owe your query a completion just because it has already wasted your CPU cycles.

For a single server whose service times are independent exponential random variables with rate \(\mu\) (completions per unit time), the mean service time is \(\mathbb{E}[S]=1/\mu\). This is the service-time component of an \(M/M/1\) model; it is not a claim that every production service has memoryless execution time. In a stable, infinite-buffer \(M/M/1\) queue, the arrival rate must satisfy \(\lambda \lt \mu\); the queueing delay is separate from the service duration defined here. The density and survival functions are:

\[ f(t) = \mu \cdot e^{-\mu t} \quad \Big| \quad P(T > t) = e^{-\mu t} \]
Decoding the parameters:
  • \(\mu\) (Service Rate): The exponential rate, in completions per unit time. For example, \(\mu=100\) requests/s implies a mean service time of \(1/\mu=0.01\) s = 10 ms for this single-server model.
  • \(t\): The elapsed time threshold you are evaluating (\(t \ge 0\)).
  • \(P(T > t) = e^{-\mu t}\): The Survival Function—the exact probability that service takes longer than time \(t\). Note that a caller's total deadline budget encompasses queueing delay, network transit time, and any downstream work; service time is only one component. Furthermore, an upstream timeout does not automatically cancel downstream operations unless cancellation signals or deadlines are explicitly propagated.
Worked Scenario: The 30ms gRPC Deadline Assassination

Your team builds a pricing microservice for the Analytics Gateway with an average execution time of 10ms (\(\mu = 100\text{ req/s} = 0.1\text{ req/ms}\)). During an architecture review, someone proposes setting a strict upstream gRPC timeout of 30ms, arguing: "30ms is 3x the average latency! Only broken requests take 3x the average!"

Assuming exponentially distributed service times, let's see what happens when you deploy that timeout:

Mean Service Time: \(\mathbb{E}[S] = \frac{1}{\mu} = 10\text{ ms}\) (\(\mu = 0.1\text{ ms}^{-1}\))

1. How many requests take longer than the 10ms "average"?
\(P(T > 10\text{ ms}) = e^{-(0.1 \times 10)} = e^{-1} \approx \mathbf{36.79\%}\)
(Note: Median latency is \(\frac{\ln(2)}{\mu} = 6.93\text{ ms}\). Most requests are fast, a long tail pulls the mean up.)

2. How many requests exceed the 30ms gRPC deadline?
\(P(T > 30\text{ ms}) = e^{-(0.1 \times 30)} = e^{-3} \approx \mathbf{0.0498\ (4.98\%)}\)

3. Where is the actual p99 latency?
\(0.01 = e^{-0.1 \times t} \implies t = \frac{-\ln(0.01)}{0.1} = \frac{4.605}{0.1} = \mathbf{46.05\text{ ms}}\)

Interpretation: Under the exponential service-time assumption, about 4.98% of service executions exceed 30 ms, and the modelled p99 service time is about 46.05 ms. Neither figure is an end-to-end gRPC latency prediction: queueing, network transit, and downstream work also consume the deadline. Propagate deadlines and cancellation to the actual work. Retry amplification is a separate feedback mechanism treated in Part 6; retries should not be mistaken for additional Erlang stages.

Telemetry Check: Bimodal Mixture Reality (\(c_s^2\))

An exponential service-time distribution has coefficient of variation \(c_s=\sigma_S/\mathbb{E}[S]=1\) (the denominator is the mean service time, not the rate parameter). A production service may instead be a mixture: for example, 90% cache hits at 1 ms and 10% database misses at 80 ms. That mixture has a different mean and variability and should be measured as a mixture, not labelled exponential by default. Stop-the-world pauses can also correlate delays across many requests at once. Do not infer a Pareto tail merely from a few large observations; validate the tail model against representative data.

3. The Erlang Distribution (Sequential Pipelines)

The Intuition: The Microservice Relay Race

What happens when a single request has to pass sequentially through Ingress → Schema Validation → Enrichment → Partition Router → Storage Write before returning an acknowledgement? If each of those \(k\) stages has an independent, identically distributed exponential duration with service rate \(\mu\), and the request waits for all stages sequentially, the sum of stage durations follows an Erlang-\(k\) distribution. Real stages may be correlated, have different rates, overlap, or contain queueing; validate these assumptions before using the curve as a production predictor.

For \(k\) independent, sequential exponential stages that all have the same rate \(\mu\), the total duration is Erlang-\(k\): its mean is \(k/\mu\), its variance is \(k/\mu^2\), and its squared coefficient of variation is \(1/k\). The equal-rate and independence assumptions are essential. A sum of independent exponential stages with different rates is generally hypoexponential, while dependent stage times require a model that represents their dependence. If stages overlap, the elapsed time is not a simple sum. This Erlang model describes stage durations only; it does not automatically include queueing before each stage or end-to-end reliability. Parallel fan-out is a different maximum-of-latencies problem, covered in Part 4.

For \(k\) independent exponential stage durations with a common rate \(\mu\), the Erlang density and survival function are:

\[ f(x) = \frac{\mu^k \cdot x^{k-1} \cdot e^{-\mu x}}{(k-1)!} \quad \Big| \quad P(T > x) = e^{-\mu x} \sum_{n=0}^{k-1} \frac{(\mu x)^n}{n!} \]
Decoding the parameters:
  • \(k\) (Shape): The integer number of sequential hops or pipeline stages (\(k = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)).
  • \(\mu\) (Rate): The common exponential rate of each stage, in inverse time units. All stages must have the same rate and be independent for this Erlang expression to apply. The mean total duration is \(\mathbb{E}[T]=k/\mu\).
  • \(x\) (Threshold): An elapsed-time threshold with \(x\ge0\), expressed in the same time unit as \(1/\mu\).
  • Why \(P(T > x)\) is a sum: A pipeline takes longer than time \(x\) if and only if strictly fewer than \(k\) stages have completed by time \(x\).
Worked Scenario: The 3-Hop Pipeline vs. The 50ms API Gateway SLA

Your API Gateway enforces an end-to-end SLA of 50ms. To fulfill a request, your orchestrator calls 3 sequential microservices (\(k = 3\): Auth, Profile, Billing). Each service takes an average of 10ms (\(\mu = 0.1\text{ ms}^{-1}\)). The expected total latency is \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 3 / 0.1 = 30\text{ ms}\).

What percentage of user requests breach the 50ms SLA (\(x = 50\text{ ms}\))? Plugging \(\mu x = 5\) and \(k = 3\) into the Erlang Survival formula:

Dimensionless Budget: \(\mu x = 0.1 \text{ ms}^{-1} \times 50\text{ ms} = 5.0\)
Base Decay: \(e^{-5} \approx 0.006738\)

Sum terms for \(n = 0\) to \(k - 1\) (\(n = 0, 1, 2\) stages completed by 50ms):
\(n = 0\): \(\frac{5^0}{0!} = 1.0\)
\(n = 1\): \(\frac{5^1}{1!} = 5.0\)
\(n = 2\): \(\frac{5^2}{2!} = \frac{25}{2} = 12.5\)
------------------------------------------------------------
Series Sum = \(1.0 + 5.0 + 12.5 = 18.5\)

SLA Breach Probability: \(P(T > 50\text{ ms}) = 0.006738 \times 18.5 = 0.12465\) (12.47%)

Interpretation: Under the independent, equal-rate exponential-stage model, the probability that the sum of the three stage durations exceeds 50 ms is about 12.47%. This is a modelled latency-threshold exceedance, not a claim that the request fails or that a production SLA breach rate has been measured. Queueing before stages, network overhead, correlation, and differing stage distributions would change the end-to-end result.

Worked Scenario: Little’s Law and Gateway Concurrency

Our Analytics Gateway completes 500 queries/s. Its measured mean time inside the Gateway's boundary—including time waiting in the router queue and execution time—is 40ms. Little’s Law estimates the long-run average number of queries active inside that boundary:

\[ L = \lambda_{\text{eff}} W \]
Admitted Throughput: \(\lambda_{\text{eff}} = 500\text{ queries/s}\)
Mean time in system: \(W = 40\text{ ms} = 0.040\text{ s}\)

\(L = 500 \times 0.040 = \mathbf{20\ queries}\)

The result means an average of 20 queries are in flight within that measured boundary. It does not mean 20 threads, database connections, or downstream calls: async I/O, fan-out, connection pooling, and per-request resource use fundamentally alter those relationships. Furthermore, Little's Law demands long-run stability, meaning \(\lambda_{\text{eff}}\) is the throughput of admitted requests, not the raw arrival rate if dropping occurs. Applying it to short, non-stationary micro-bursts without boundary alignment fails completely.

The Grand Unification: Kingman’s Approximation & The Utilization Trap

To see how variability and utilization affect waiting, consider one serial processing lane—for example, one hot Azure Event Hubs partition handled by one effective processing lane in the Gateway. Kingman’s formula is a mean queue-wait approximation for a stable, single-server \(G/G/1\) queue with a stationary renewal arrival process and an independent, identically distributed service-time sequence with finite mean and variance; the arrival and service sequences are assumed independent, under the usual heavy-traffic approximation. It is not a p95 or p99 predictor, and it is not a direct model of a pooled multi-server \(G/G/c\) worker pool. For an idealized \(M/M/c\) pool, Erlang-C is a standard model; more general multi-server systems require an appropriate approximation or simulation.

  • 1. Illustrative scenario: During peak trading hours, lag rises on Event Hubs Partition 7 while the consumer process appears to have spare CPU capacity at the pod level. The 90% figure used later is a model input, not a universal threshold or a measured incident.
  • 2. Architectural assumption: "There is 10% headroom, so this partition's backlog should not grow." That conclusion is valid only if the effective service capacity of the lane exceeds its own arrival rate.
  • 3. Physical mechanism: Partition 7 receives its own arrival process \(\lambda_7\) and is served by an effective lane with rate \(\mu_7\). Bursty inter-arrivals increase \(c_a^2\); variable processing times caused by payload size or storage contention increase \(c_s^2\). Aggregate arrival rate across all partitions can conceal this local imbalance.
  • 4. Mathematical model: For this one lane, \(\rho=\lambda_7/\mu_7\), where \(\lambda_7\) is the mean arrival rate and \(\mu_7=1/\mathbb{E}[S]\) is the effective service rate. The squared coefficients of variation are \(c_a^2=\operatorname{Var}(A)/\mathbb{E}[A]^2\) and \(c_s^2=\operatorname{Var}(S)/\mathbb{E}[S]^2\). Kingman estimates the mean queue wait \(\mathbb{E}[W_q]\).

5. The illustrative calculation:

Assume mean service time \(\mathbb{E}[S]=10\text{ ms}\), stable utilization \(\rho=0.90\), and \(c_a=c_s=1\). These are illustrative inputs representing exponential-like variability, not measurements from a production partition. Kingman’s approximation gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[W_q] &\approx \left(\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\right) \left(\frac{c_a^2 + c_s^2}{2}\right) \mathbb{E}[S] \\ &= \left(\frac{0.9}{1 - 0.9}\right) \left(\frac{1^2 + 1^2}{2}\right) (10\text{ ms}) \\ &= 9 \cdot 1 \cdot 10 = \mathbf{90\text{ ms}} \end{aligned} \]

If a slight traffic bump pushes utilization to 95% (\(\rho = 0.95\)), holding the other assumptions constant, the denominator \((1 - \rho)\) shrinks rapidly:

\[ \mathbb{E}[W_q] \approx \left(\frac{0.95}{0.05}\right) \cdot 1 \cdot 10 = \mathbf{190\text{ ms}} \]

The mean service time remains 10 ms, while the estimated mean queue wait rises from 90 ms to 190 ms. Kingman does not predict the p99 from these inputs. As \(\rho\to1\), the approximation grows without bound; for an infinite-buffer single-server queue, \(\rho\ge1\) means no finite steady-state queue exists. A finite buffer behaves differently: occupancy remains bounded, but rejection or backpressure increases.

The calculation also shows why no utilization percentage is a universal threshold. Holding the same 10 ms mean service time and \(c_a=c_s=1\), the approximation gives about 23.3 ms mean queue wait at 70% utilization, 40 ms at 80%, 90 ms at 90%, and 190 ms at 95%. With lower variability, the wait can be much smaller at the same utilization; with burstier arrivals or heavier service-time variability, it can be larger. Capacity decisions must include the required latency objective, arrival shape, service variability, burst duration, and recovery headroom.

6. Architectural decision: First determine whether the bottleneck is per partition or aggregate. Reduce the hot-lane arrival rate through partitioning or workload routing where ordering and key semantics permit; increase effective service capacity; reduce service-time variability by bounding payload and query work; and use bounded queues with explicit admission/backpressure policies. Jitter can desynchronize scheduled producers, but it does not turn arbitrary traffic into a Poisson process. Each control has a cost: more partitions affect ordering and operations, reserved capacity costs money, and shedding sacrifices work by design.

7. Production verification: Measure per-partition inter-arrival times and service times, estimate squared coefficients of variation from representative samples, and track utilization, queue depth, oldest-event age, admitted throughput, rejected work, and drain rate. Compare the modelled mean wait with measured mean wait under representative stable periods; use empirical quantiles or a validated simulation for tail objectives. Re-run the analysis after workload, partition assignment, or admission policy changes.

Production Checklist: Validate Before You Size

Mathematical queueing models and approximations are useful for capacity planning and root-cause analysis, but they are built on assumptions. Before making production architectural or scaling decisions based on these models, validate them against actual telemetry:

  • Arrival counts across multiple window sizes: Verify whether arrival counts follow Poisson variance (\(I \approx 1\)) or exhibit overdispersion (\(I \gg 1\)) due to cron jobs, batching, or unjittered retries.
  • Inter-arrival and service-time distributions: Check whether service times match exponential assumptions or reveal bimodal structures (e.g., cache hits vs. database misses) or heavy tails.
  • Queue-depth and rejection telemetry: Monitor point-in-time queue occupancy, admission drop counters, and queue wait times directly rather than relying solely on CPU utilization gauges.
  • Out-of-sample validation: Test model predictions against traffic spikes during peak load or degraded operating conditions. Goodness-of-fit during calm periods does not prove stability under overload.
  • Test controls, not only distributions: verify bounded queue length and age, deadline propagation, cancellation, admission/rejection behavior, retry budgets, and recovery/drain time under sustained overload.

Decision rule: prefer the simplest model that is mechanistically plausible, fits representative telemetry—including the tails relevant to the SLO—and changes an engineering decision. Revisit it when workload, topology, or controls change.

Queueing theory explains how arrival variability, service variability, utilization, and finite capacity shape waiting and recovery. It also forces an important distinction: aggregate worker capacity does not guarantee that every partition or tenant has enough service capacity. The next part changes the question from sequential sums and queue waits to parallel aggregation: when a request fans out across many partitions and waits for the slowest response, order statistics and tail behaviour become the governing mathematics. That is the focus of Part 4.
